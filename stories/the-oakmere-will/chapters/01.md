## Chapter 1. The Coach on Friday

The circulating library at Amberlight was one room above the draper's, with a stove that smoked and eleven feet of shelf, and it was understood in the parish that nothing was properly known until it had been said there. Miss Judith Vane was returning the second volume of a novel she had not much liked when the stairs began to complain, which meant Mrs Pike.

**Mrs Pike** “Old Mr Fenner is dead. Oakmere Farm. A fortnight since, and buried these ten days, and not one of us told until this morning.”

She said it with the particular satisfaction of a woman who has carried a thing up a flight of stairs and found the right people at the top of it. Mr Rowe, the curate, who had been reaching for a sermon volume nobody else ever borrowed, put his hand down again.

**Mr Rowe** “A fortnight. But there was no—that is, I should have been asked to—was there no service at all, then?”

**Mrs Pike** “At Alresford, with his cousin's people, and the whole thing over before a soul here had it. Not so much as a Fenner over from Bramdean, and the mason his own second cousin. And the young man is in the house already. Mr Vernon Hartwell, that is. Curtains up, a man of his own in the yard, and boots I am told cost four guineas.”

**Miss Vane** “In the house already.”

**Mrs Pike** “Under a will. Drawn in September, my dear, three weeks before the old man died, and everything to Mr Hartwell. Land, stock, the hop ground, the whole three hundred a year of it. Witnessed by two of Fenner's own servants, which I call very proper, for who else was there?”

Judith set the novel on the counter and did not let go of it. She had a memory, uncomfortably clear, of Aldous Fenner standing in her brother's shop with his hat in his hand, saying that the farm was for the boy and always had been, and that he did not care to hear another word about it.

**Miss Vane** “Then it cuts out Captain Farr.”

**Mrs Pike** “It cuts out nobody. You cannot cut out a dead man.”

**Miss Vane** “He was reported dead. That is not the same thing.”

**Mrs Pike** “Three years, Miss Vane. Three years and not a line. A living officer writes to his own great-uncle, or he writes to somebody, and I have had no letter and Mr Rowe has had no letter and the old man had none neither, for he told me so himself on this very green. Depend upon it. The poor young man is buried abroad and Mr Hartwell has the farm and there is an end.”

It was a Friday, and Friday and Tuesday are the coach days, so that the horn came up through the floor and put a stop to the conversation as it had put a stop to a hundred others. Mrs Pike was at the window first. The library window looks north up the lane and takes in the corner of the green, where the coach turns in under the Bell sign, and that is the reason a great many people borrow books.

**Mrs Pike** “Two inside and one on the roof. Nobody I know.”

The man off the roof came down stiffly, favouring one leg, and stood a moment in the mud with his bag. His coat had been a regimental coat once. He was burned brown to the wrists in October, which no Hampshire man is, and he asked the ostler something, and the ostler pointed east along the Alresford road.

**Miss Vane** “What did he say? Mrs Pike. What did he ask?”

**Mrs Pike** “The way to Oakmere Farm. Though why a stranger should want—”

She stopped. Judith had already gone down the stairs.
