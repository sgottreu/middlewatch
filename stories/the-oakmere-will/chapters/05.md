## Chapter 5. The Road to Winchester

Owen Vane gave up his dispensing book upon one condition, stated once: that it be back on his counter by Friday, because half the parish's physic was written in it and he would not dispense from memory for anybody. Judith put it under her arm and walked down to the Bell before the light went.

She found Captain Farr in the parlour, and Miss Hester Lamb with him, who had walked over from Upper Barton with a basket of eggs he had not asked for and was taking him to task about the fire he had not lit. He heard Judith through without once interrupting, which was not a thing she was used to. When she came to the letter he set down his cup.

**Captain Farr** “April. She wrote in April, and burnt two before she got one to please her, and I was at Lisbon in April doing nothing whatever but learning to walk again.”

**Miss Lamb** “I read it. She brought it to our kitchen in April and I spelt the regiment for her, and she blotted the outside with her thumb and cried over the second sheet. I never asked what became of it. I thought it had gone.”

**Miss Vane** “She had no direction but the regiment.”

**Captain Farr** “The regiment would have found me. It found me with a bill from a bootmaker in Ipswich. And she gave it into his hand?”

**Miss Vane** “At the kitchen table, with the rest of the post. He had taken charge of it in March.”

**Captain Farr** “Then I want Cobbold. His clerk drew the September will and his office has held my great-uncle's papers since before I was breeched. If there was ever an earlier one, it is on his shelf, and he will know it. I shall walk in Tuesday.”

**Miss Vane** “You will not walk eleven miles on that leg to be told to come back Thursday.”

Mr Rowe had come in behind her with the subscription book still under his coat, and had been standing by the settle working himself up to something for a full minute.

**Mr Rowe** “Forgive me. Captain, forgive me, but you will go in alone, and you are a soldier nobody has seen these seven years, with no money, and a claim to three hundred a year. Any attorney in England hears that story twice a month. It is always told by a man who wants the farm.”

**Captain Farr** “I do want the farm.”

**Mr Rowe** “Yes. That is precisely the trouble with it. But if Miss Vane went, and the book were in her keeping and not yours, and she said, my brother wrote this line and will swear to it, that is not a convenient story. That is a ledger in the hand of somebody who gets nothing whatever by it. And Miss Lamb, who read the letter in April and will say so.”

He stopped, appalled at himself, and looked at the floor. Judith looked at him for rather longer than he liked.

**Miss Vane** “That is the first sensible thing anybody has said since Friday. I will go.”

**Miss Lamb** “And so shall I. It is my spelling on the outside of it.”

Mrs Pike had it by nine the next morning, being at the Bell about a parcel she was not expecting, and gave her opinion in the yard where it would carry.

**Mrs Pike** “Eleven miles, in a hired chaise, two young women and a gentleman this parish has not clapped eyes on since the year nine. My dear, I do not doubt him and I do not doubt either of you, and that is nothing at all to the purpose. It is what is said. Send the curate.”

**Miss Vane** “Mr Rowe cannot swear to my brother's hand.”

**Mrs Pike** “Then send Owen.”

**Miss Vane** “Owen would shut the shop and go, ma'am, and half the parish would be without its physic till he came back, and I will not have it. Ma'am, I am six and twenty and I keep house for an apothecary. There is very little left of me to be ruined that Amberlight has not already had the pleasure of.”

Mr Rowe paid for the chaise out of two pounds he had by him for the vestry glazing, and said the glazier could wait until Lady Day, and would not be thanked for it.

They went at ten. The road was cut up from the week's rain and the near hind wheel had been complaining since the Alresford turning, and about three miles short of the city it stopped complaining and went. The chaise came down slowly and sideways into the ditch grass, and the dispensing book slid off Judith's lap into the straw, and Hester caught it, and nobody was hurt at all.

The driver got the horses' heads and walked back to look at what was left of the felloe.

**Captain Farr** “Can it be got on again?”

The driver crouched, turned a splinter of the rim over in his fingers, and threw it into the ditch.

**Captain Farr** “Then what passes this road tonight?”

The driver straightened up and looked west, where the light was already going out of the fields. Nothing passed here now, he said, till the market carts in the morning. The wheelwright at Itchen Vale was a mile and a half off, and would not come out after dark for any money in Hampshire.
