## Chapter 8. Oakmere Reclaimed

The horse cost fifteen shillings and was not worth eight, and they came into Amberlight at half past ten at night with Cobbold's copy of the letter buttoned inside Farr's coat and Cobbold's own note under it, three lines requiring Mr Vernon Hartwell to attend at Winchester and account for the September instrument. Mr Rowe was knocked up at six. He came out with his neckcloth in his hand and did not ask a single question until they were past the church.

They were shown into the front parlour, where the yellow curtains were, at eight o'clock. Hartwell had been at his breakfast and brought his napkin with him. Mrs Whitlow stood in the doorway because Judith had asked her to and would not sit.

**Mr Hartwell** “Well. Three of you, and the housekeeper. This has the air of a deputation.”

Farr put three things on the table in the order they had agreed. Owen Vane's dispensing book, open at September. A half sheet in Judith's hand, being what Mrs Whitlow had said in her own kitchen. And the letter.

**Captain Farr** “Nineteenth of April. Directed to my regiment, returned into Hampshire, and lying on Cobbold's back shelf since the summer. You may read what she wrote about him. He did not know the dairy maid in April, and your will is dated the fourteenth of September.”

**Mr Hartwell** “A letter may go astray a hundred ways. I am sure I never saw it.”

**Mrs Whitlow** “You had it off me at the kitchen table. You said it should go with the rest on Friday. You had the post from March, sir, and everything in this house went out under your hand, and I have not slept a night since he came off that coach.”

**Mr Hartwell** “The woman is sixty and confused.”

**Mrs Whitlow** “I am fifty-four.”

**Mr Rowe** “I shall write down what has been said here, and I shall sign it, and so will Miss Vane, and I am sorry for it, but I find I am not sorry at all, which I daresay I ought to examine later.”

Hartwell looked at the note from Cobbold for a long time, and did not read it twice, because he had understood it the first time. He asked, in a lighter voice, what was wanted.

**Captain Farr** “Thursday's sale cried off before noon. The stock left where it stands. And you out of my great-uncle's house before dark.”

He went at four. He took the yellow curtains, which nobody grudged him, and left the dog, which had never been his. The chaise from the Bell carried him as far as Alresford and his debts in town carried him the rest of the way, and Amberlight has not seen him since.

Mrs Pike had the whole affair over the parish inside a day, and improved it.

**Mrs Pike** “I said in the library on the very Friday that the thing smelt wrong. Boots at four guineas and a will three weeks old. Depend upon it, I said. And so it has proved, and I am glad somebody in this parish keeps her eyes open.”

Judith let her have it. It cost nothing, and Mrs Pike had told her about the coach.

On the Sunday, Farr walked her back as far as the Oakmere gate, which is not on the way to anywhere she was going.

**Captain Farr** “Three hundred a year, and the hop ground, and a roof that wants doing. I could not have asked you in October with eleven pounds and a chalk score at the Bell. I am asking now, and properly, and you may take a week over it.”

**Miss Vane** “I shall not want a week. I wore no black for you and I kept your book instead, and I am seven and twenty and tired of saying less than I think. Yes, Captain. And you may come and get Anson out from under my bed yourself, for I have carried him quite far enough.”
