## Chapter 7. The Attorney's Pigeonhole

The wheelwright came out at seven, grumbled at the felloe, and had them into Winchester by half past ten. Mr Cobbold's office is up two steps off the close, with a fire that is lit at nine whether anybody wants it or not, and a clerk who writes standing.

Cobbold heard the whole of it without moving his hands from the arms of his chair. He had drawn papers for Aldous Fenner for thirty years, he said, and had buried three of his own clients that quarter, and he was too old to be surprised by a farm.

On the point of the earlier will he was perfectly clear. There was one, made in the year eight, leaving Oakmere to Fenner's great-nephew Nathaniel Farr. It had never been revoked. Nobody had burnt it, nobody had cancelled it, no codicil touched it. It had simply been set aside as superseded by a later paper, which is what any office does; and if the later paper should prove to be no will at all, then the earlier one stood exactly where it had stood for eight years.

**Captain Farr** “Then it wants only that the September paper be broken.”

Only, said Cobbold, was a large word for it. He turned over Owen Vane's dispensing book and read the entry for the fourteenth twice, and said it was good as far as it went and that it did not go far enough.

**Miss Vane** “Then let me ask you something else, sir. A letter goes out of Oakmere in April, directed to a regiment that has marched, in a poor hand. It cannot be delivered. Where does the Post Office put it?”

Cobbold said that depended on what else was written upon it. A letter with nothing but a wrong direction went to the dead letter office and was destroyed at the year's end. A letter that carried the sender's own place upon it, Oakmere Farm, Amberlight, was another matter; it was sent back into the county, and anything belonging to Fenner had come to this office these thirty years, because everybody in Hampshire knew who held his papers.

He rang for the clerk. There was a shelf, he said, in the back room, of correspondence they could not put a claimant to. Nobody threw such things away. Nobody read them either, they not being addressed to anybody in the office, and there were four years of it.

The shelf was seven pigeonholes wide. Judith took the first three and Farr the rest, and it went quicker than either expected, because most of what is unclaimed is bills.

She found it in the second hole from the end, three sheets folded thick, the seal unbroken, the direction crossed out twice by two different hands and marked in the corner: not known with the regiment. Under that, in a large laboured hand, the writer had put her own place at the head of the sheet as a careful servant does.

**Miss Vane** “The nineteenth of April. Sir, will you break it? I will not have it said that we opened it ourselves.”

Cobbold broke it, and read it at the window, and his face did not alter, which Judith thought said a great deal about thirty years of practice. Then he read the material part aloud. Dorcas Whitlow's spelling was poor and her meaning was not. The master went about the house at night and called for his brother that was dead. He did not know the dairy maid. He had signed nothing for a month because his hand would not do it, and Mr Hartwell had the post now, and the captain had best come home directly.

April. Five months before any clerk carried a fresh will out along the Alresford road.

**Captain Farr** “It was sent. He sent it.”

**Miss Vane** “He sent it where it could not arrive. That is worse, and it is also written down.”

Cobbold laid the sheets flat and put a paperweight on them. He would write to Mr Hartwell today, he said, requiring his attendance to account for the September instrument; and he advised them to say nothing to anybody until the letter was in the man's hand.

They came out into the close at one o'clock. Pasted on the shutter of the corn chandler's at the bottom of the hill was a bill, still damp, announcing a sale by auction at Oakmere Farm upon Thursday next: forty ewes, the dairy stock, the waggons, and the standing hop poles.

**Captain Farr** “Thursday. He is selling everything that walks.”

**Miss Vane** “Then the letter will not do it alone, and we cannot wait upon the post. Get us a horse, Captain. We go back tonight.”
