## Chapter 4. What Mrs Whitlow Knew

The parish alms at Amberlight amount to seven pounds a year and a bushel of coal at Christmas, and are dispensed by whoever the curate can persuade to walk with him. Mr Rowe carried the book. Miss Vane carried the basket, which had in it a jar of blackberry jelly nobody at Oakmere could possibly need, and on the mile out along the Alresford road he said three times that he was not at all sure this was honest.

**Mr Rowe** “Because we are going for one thing under the name of another, you see, and that is—well, it is not lying precisely, the alms book is genuine, I have it here, but if a man asked me plainly what I had come for I should have to—”

**Miss Vane** “Then we must hope he does not ask you plainly.”

Mr Hartwell received them in the hall, which was cold, and did not take them out of it. He looked at the alms book for rather longer than a man looks at a thing he means to read.

**Mr Hartwell** “Five shillings, then, and put me down for the coal as well. My poor cousin gave nothing, I think? No. He was a close old gentleman, and I do not say it against him.”

**Mr Rowe** “He gave a guinea at Easter. Every Easter for thirty years.”

**Mr Hartwell** “Did he indeed. Well, you see how little I know the place yet.”

Judith said that she had brought something for Mrs Whitlow, who had been unwell, and asked whether she might step through to the kitchen. Hartwell's smile arrived before he had decided upon it. He said the house was at sixes and sevens; he said it twice; and then Mr Rowe began, at some length and with great earnestness, upon the difficulty of the roof over the north aisle, and Judith walked down the passage before anybody could think of a reason to stop her.

Mrs Whitlow was scouring a pan that did not need it. She had the keys at her waist and her hands would not be still, and when she saw who it was she looked past Judith's shoulder at the door.

**Mrs Whitlow** “He was in the parlour all that morning, Miss Vane, and quite himself, and he called for the paper and signed it and knew what he signed.”

**Miss Vane** “I had not asked you anything.”

The pan went down on the table. Judith set the jelly beside it and sat, unasked, on the settle by the fire, which is a thing that keeps a frightened woman standing for only about a minute.

**Mrs Whitlow** “I am told what to say. I am not a fool, miss. I am fifty-four and I have my place and nothing else in the world, and I was told the words of it and I have said them to three people this week.”

**Miss Vane** “Then say something you were not told.”

The old woman looked at the passage door. When she spoke again her voice had dropped so far that Judith leaned toward it.

**Mrs Whitlow** “In the spring. April, or thereabouts, when the master began going about the house at night and calling for his brother that has been dead since the year six. I wrote to the boy. To the captain. I got the regiment out of an old letter in the master's desk and I wrote it myself, three sheets, and my spelling is poor but it was plain enough. I said his great-uncle was failing and he had best come home.”

**Miss Vane** “Where did you take it?”

**Mrs Whitlow** “Nowhere. Mr Hartwell had the post by then. He came in February and he had the post from March, all of it, the letters out and the letters in, for the master could not read a bill by then and somebody must. I gave it into his hand in this kitchen. He said it should go with the rest on Friday.”

Somewhere above them a door opened and shut. Mrs Whitlow picked up the pan again.

**Miss Vane** “And did it go?”

**Mrs Whitlow** “I never saw it after. I never saw it in the bag and I never saw it burnt, and I have thought about it every night since the captain came off that coach, and I cannot tell you, miss. That is the whole of it. I cannot tell you whether it was ever sent at all.”
