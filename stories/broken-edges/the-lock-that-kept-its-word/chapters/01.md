## Chapter 1. Six Days Before the Pilings

The morning's salvage lay in two rows on the Floatsome platform, dripping through the gaps between boards. Jory Brennick had brought up a brass window catch, nine spoons, and a cook pot with a hole in it. Liora Quill had brought up a sounding bell off some drowned ferry, and she had set it where the sun would hit it.

**Liora Quill** “Nine spoons.”

She turned one over with her toe.

**Liora Quill** “You'll feed a family with that. Not this year, but eventually.”

**Jory Brennick** “Spoons sell. Nobody in Hartsford is short of bells.”

**Liora Quill** “Nobody in Hartsford is short of holes in pots, either, and you brought them one.”

A hired skiff came in along the outer walkway, rowed by a Shoreside boy who clearly wanted paying and gone. The passenger stood up too early and had to grab the post. He was an old man in a patched wool coat with a leather apron under it, and a brass loupe swinging on a cord at his throat. He asked the dockhands for Jory Brennick. Not for the Brennicks. For Jory, by name.

Liora raised both eyebrows and said nothing, which was worse.

**Walter Ames** “I'm sorry to come at you over your work. My name is Walter Ames. I keep the herb shop up on Cutler Street in Hartsford, and I have a letter I'd like you to read.”

He unwrapped it from oilcloth. The paper had been wet once and dried badly, and the ink had bloomed at the folds.

**Walter Ames** “My grandmother wrote it. Six or seven months after the water came, when nobody yet believed the town was gone for good. She says she put something in the cellar of our shop, down in the old streets, and sealed it there. I only found the letter last month. My aunt died in the spring and somebody had to sort her boxes.”

**Jory Brennick** “Which street?”

**Walter Ames** “Kettle Row. The short end of it, where the dyers were.”

Jory crouched over the letter without touching it. Liora came and stood behind his shoulder, pretending she had not.

**Walter Ames** “There's a difficulty. The Shoreside crew is driving new pilings into that stretch in six days. Once they do, the cellar entrance is under their footings, and it stays there. I'd not have troubled you if I had a year. I have six days.”

Maud Brennick had come along the walkway with a coil of line over her arm, and had stopped some while back. Jory had not seen her stop. He never did.

**Maud Brennick** “Kettle Row sits two hundred paces off the fissure, give or take how honest you are about paces. Nobody's surveyed it since the tremor loosened the harbour shelf, and that was four years ago. Half those roofs went down and nobody wrote which half.”

**Jory Brennick** “Then it's six days to find out.”

He straightened and put out his hand to Walter Ames, who looked at it a moment before taking it, as though he had expected to spend longer asking.

**Jory Brennick** “I'll take the contract. Full rate, and I don't promise the cellar's still a cellar.”

**Walter Ames** “That's fair. That's more than fair.”

Maud set the coil of line down on the boards, deliberately, where everyone could see it.

**Maud Brennick** “Then I'm coming. My chart of that street is older than the tremor, and that makes it the only one anybody has. It'll be wrong. But it'll be wrong in ways I can read.”
