## Chapter 5. What Maud Remembers

That evening on the Floatsome platform, Jory Brennick coiled dive line and said it over to himself again. The household name, three times, in open air. Never a word of it under the water.

Maud Brennick sat across from him with a torn net over her knees and a shuttle in her hand.

**Maud Brennick** “And the plate was warm.”

**Jory Brennick** “Through a wet glove. I've had my hand on cold iron down there and it takes the feeling out of you in a minute. That plate gave it back.”

The shuttle stopped halfway through a mesh. Maud looked past her son at the dark water beyond the lashed hulls.

**Maud Brennick** “I was two-and-twenty the autumn after the flood. We were in the camp above the shoreline then, the one that burned twice. Boats went out over the old streets all that season. Everybody rowing out to float over their own roof and see what the water had left of it.”

**Jory Brennick** “Salvaging.”

**Maud Brennick** “Grieving, mostly. They stopped by the turn of the year. Cold came in, and there's nothing to bring up but furniture. By midwinter the streets had nobody over them at all. Except one. An old woman rowed out alone, three and four nights a week, with a lantern standing in her bow. Kettle Row, the short end, over the dyers. I know the corner because I used to watch the light sit still on the water. A lantern on a boat moves. Hers didn't. She'd tie up and work, and the light would hold in one place half the night.”

**Jory Brennick** “Nobody asked her what she was at?”

**Maud Brennick** “People asked. She wasn't a woman who answered. What they said about her instead was the fever.”

Liora Quill had come along the walkway with her boots in her hand, and stopped where she was.

**Maud Brennick** “It went through the camps that first winter and took the old and the small ones first. Then it stopped taking them. The talk was of a draught going round. The talk was that it came off an old woman out of the drowned town. Children who'd gone grey in the face sat up and asked for bread. I never drank it. My mother did.”

**Liora Quill** “And nobody wrote down how she made it?”

**Maud Brennick** “Nobody got the chance. She stopped coming round in the spring. One season she was rowing out. The next she wasn't, and people had roofs of their own to put on.”

Jory looked at the coil in his hands and did not finish it.

**Maud Brennick** “Here's what I want you holding tomorrow. That stone came up out of the seabed six months before she worked it. Nobody knew what it was. Nobody knows now. She made that lock once and shut it once, and no hand has opened it since. Whatever it takes off the one who speaks, there's not a living soul who has watched it taken.”

**Jory Brennick** “Walter Ames knows that. He read it to us himself.”

**Maud Brennick** “Reading a thing and standing on a wet deck saying your own name to it are different trades.”

Feet came along the outer walkway at a run. A Shoreside boy, soaked to the knee, with his cap screwed up in his fist. "Mistress Vance says the storm's turned in the night. The frame comes out of the yard tomorrow. Pilings go in two days."

**Liora Quill** “Two days.”

Maud set the shuttle down on the boards.

**Maud Brennick** “Then we go at first light, the three of us. Your old man stays in the boat with his letter.”
