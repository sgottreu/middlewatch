## Chapter 8. What the Chest Was For

They laid the black-bound book open on the counter in Walter Ames's shop that afternoon, with the wrapped stone set at the far end where nobody's sleeve would reach it. Walter's right hand was bound in flannel and no use to him. So Jory Brennick turned the pages, and read out the lines the tide-mark had spared.

**Jory Brennick** “Fourth trial. Stone ground to a flour, and the weight of it is gone, that's all brown. Then to the pint of water off the boil. Steep until cold. A spoon at the turn of the fever and a spoon at dawn.”

He pressed the page flat with the side of his hand.

**Jory Brennick** “She wrote the weight out again in the margin. Three times over, in three different inks.”

**Walter Ames** “She burned three sets of measures before this one. She wouldn't trust her own hand on a wet morning.”

**Liora Quill** “Can you read it or can't you?”

**Jory Brennick** “I can read it. It's a hair under the weight on the page before. A hair, and she's underlined the hair.”

**Walter Ames** “Then it matters.”

Walter shut the book with his left hand and kept it shut.

**Walter Ames** “I've ground herbs forty years and no one has ever asked me to be true to a grain. The woman at the fever house above the cistern has scales that will weigh a gnat. She gets the book, and she gets the stone, and she handles both. I'd sooner be the man who carried it than the man who spoiled it.”

* * *

In the morning Jory walked up Cutler Street with him and stood at the fever house door while Walter knocked. A woman in a grey apron opened it with her sleeves pushed past the elbow. Walter set the book and the sailcloth bundle on her step and began to say where they had come from.

**Walter Ames** “These were my grandmother's. She kept the apothecary's shop on Kettle Row, under the water now, and her name was—”

He stopped. He tried once more, and the word was not in him. Jory watched a man of sixty-one stand on a doorstep with his mouth open and nothing coming out of it.

**Walter Ames** “I can't. I'm sorry. Read her letter. It's her hand, and she signs it, and that's better proof than my saying so.”

* * *

Three days later a girl came down to the Floatsome landing with a message and a basket of pears. Eleven in the fever house had taken the draught, measured against the old book. Nine were sitting up. The woman in the grey apron wanted more of the stone and wanted to know who had pulled it off the bottom.

That same morning Selah Vance set her hammer frame over Kettle Row and drove the first piling into the cleared street, on her schedule, with the weather still out past the point. Jory heard it from the platform. It came across the water like somebody beating a drum under a blanket.

Walter Ames paid him that evening, in coin, counted out on the boards.

**Walter Ames** “That's your full rate. This other is for your mother, for remembering a lantern that didn't move. Nobody else did.”

**Maud Brennick** “I'll take it. I've been paid in fish for nine years.”

Liora Quill sat on the edge of the platform with her heels over the water, turning one of the pears.

**Liora Quill** “She wants more of the stone. Out past the dyers' yard, where the floor's cracked open.”

**Jory Brennick** “Two hundred paces off Kettle Row. I was that close to it on Tuesday.”

**Liora Quill** “And nobody's ever dived it.”

Maud said nothing for a while. She had her chart of Kettle Row across her knees, the one that had been honest and wrong, and she was inking the gap in the wall where no gap had been drawn.

**Maud Brennick** “Not this time. You went down a street I'd charted and the street had moved. That crack has never been charted at all.”

**Jory Brennick** “So you'll say no.”

**Maud Brennick** “I'll say I go first, with a line and a slate, and I draw it before either of you so much as wets a glove. Then you can take the contract on your own name.”
