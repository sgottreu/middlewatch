## Chapter 7. A Proposal Not Required

The lych gate stands at the top of the church path, and anybody crossing the green can see who is standing under it. Judith had chosen the spot on purpose. She had her list in her hand, folded, though she had no use for it now.

**Miss Vane** “I have written out what happened. The hour we set off, the place the wheel went, Nye's name and the hurdles, and the hour we came in. I mean to say it at the library counter tomorrow with Mrs Hale standing there, and then to Mrs Yardley, and then to Sarah Copp's mother, who is the one that put the crying in.”

**Mr Rowe** “They will not believe it.”

**Miss Vane** “Some of them will. And the rest will have to say it to my face or not at all, and I would rather be laughed at in front of me than pitied behind me. I know what it will cost. I shall stand in a shop and explain a cracked spoke to fools, and be very civil about it, and go home and be sick with temper for two days. I have done it before.”

He had been holding his hat. He put it on the gate post, which was the nearest thing to putting down a shield that a curate can manage.

**Mr Rowe** “I do not want it corrected.”

**Miss Vane** “I beg your pardon?”

**Mr Rowe** “I would rather it were made true. There is no dinner arranged round this, and nobody has told me what neckcloth to wear, and Mrs Pike does not know I am standing here. I am asking whether you would have me. Eighty a year, and no expectations, and two rooms over Mrs Copp's that look at a wall.”

Judith did not answer at once. She had been asked nothing at all in seven-and-twenty years, and she had promised herself walking up the hill that she would not be grateful.

**Miss Vane** “Answer me one thing first, and answer it exactly. Do you ask because you want me, or because it would tidy the parish up? Because a man may be very kind and still be only mending a wheel.”

**Mr Rowe** “In November you told me it was Chronicles.”

**Miss Vane** “That is not an answer.”

**Mr Rowe** “It is the whole of it. I have thought about that morning perhaps three hundred times since. I told myself I was nursing an injury. A man does not go over the same half minute for eight months out of injury, Miss Vane, he does it because he cannot leave off looking at the person in it. And I have thought of that list on the green nearly as often, and every time I have wanted to go back and take it, and read the six, and come and tell you what I thought of them. That is not tidiness. Tidiness would have been Miss Marchmont, and I could not think of a thing to say to her but the weather.”

Judith laughed. It got away from her entirely, and she put the folded paper up against her mouth, and it did not help.

**Miss Vane** “You said the rain was seasonable.”

**Mr Rowe** “I did.”

**Miss Vane** “Four days out of seven.”

**Mr Rowe** “Judith.”

**Miss Vane** “Yes. Yes, I will have you, and you may stop turning your hat about, because it is on the gate post.”

It was a little after five, and the sun was still well up over the mill. She took the folded paper and put it in her pocket unread by anybody, and he took his hat, and they came down the church path and out onto the green side by side, at a walking pace, in the full light of a Monday afternoon.

The library window was bright above the draper's, and there was somebody in it.

**Miss Vane** “Do not hurry.”

**Mr Rowe** “I had not the least intention of it.”
