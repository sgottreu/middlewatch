## Chapter 3. An Evening Arranged

Mrs Pike had settled everything but the guest, and the guest arrived in the same brown she had travelled down in, with her boots wet to the ankle from cutting across the glebe rather than coming round by the road. She apologised for the boots. She did not apologise for the brown.

**Miss Marchmont** “Miss Vane. Is it true there are books still shut up in the long room at the house? Mrs Copp says there are, and Mrs Copp says a great many things.”

**Miss Vane** “There were four hundred volumes catalogued when Sir Edward died. Nobody has been in to count them since.”

**Miss Marchmont** “Four hundred. And the damp getting at every one of them.”

This was said before she had been introduced to anybody, and it was some minutes before Mrs Pike could get her turned round to face the curate at all.

Mr Rowe had been coached in the passage for a quarter of an hour and had come to table with all of it still in him. He praised the ham. He praised the roses, which he had himself cut, and which he praised as though he had found them growing on the table. He said that the prospect from the gatehouse windows was uncommonly fine, and then that the prospect from the green was uncommonly fine, and then, having exhausted the prospects, that the weather had been remarkably seasonable for the time of year.

**Miss Marchmont** “It has rained four days out of seven since I came.”

**Mr Rowe** “Seasonably.”

Miss Marchmont said that no doubt it was, and turned her attention to her plate with perfect civility. Owen Vane looked at his sister down the length of the cloth and did not permit himself anything so useful as an expression.

It was the second course before anything happened. Miss Marchmont asked what the library above the draper's kept, and Judith said that it kept whatever three subscribers had asked for last, and that at present it kept a great deal of The Orphan of Kerrow.

**Miss Marchmont** “In three volumes. She faints in the first chapter.”

**Miss Vane** “She faints in the first chapter and is not entirely upright again until the middle of the third.”

**Miss Marchmont** “And the uncle. Who says nothing whatever for a hundred pages and then leaves her an abbey.”

They laughed, both at once, in the way of two people who have found the same thing ridiculous in separate counties. From the head of the table Mrs Pike observed the laughter, observed Mr Rowe seated on the same side of the room as the laughter, and drew her conclusion.

**Mrs Pike** “There. You see. The evening is a success, and I said it would be, and I shall say so tomorrow.”

Afterwards, waiting on the step for the maid with the lantern, Miss Marchmont was less polite and a great deal better company.

**Miss Marchmont** “Mrs Pike believes I am to be courted. I am not. My uncle means to sell the house or leave it shut, and I have a fortnight to give him a reason to do neither. So tell me the truth, since you seem to be the only person here who says things once. What does this parish actually want?”

**Miss Vane** “A schoolroom. The one we have has a beam cracked through with the damp, and Fenner at Bramdean wants fifteen pounds to mend it, and the poor box holds four pounds and eleven shillings, of which two are spoken for. Mr Rowe has been going round the parish about it for a fortnight and has raised nine shillings and a promise of a load of straw.”

Miss Marchmont stood quite still on the step. The lantern came out, and the maid held it up, and she did not move towards it.

**Miss Marchmont** “How many children?”
