## Chapter 8. Subscriptions Closed

By Tuesday morning Mrs Hale had two pieces of news and one counter to tell them at, and she was seen to begin three times and change her mind twice.

**Mrs Hale** “The estate is to fit the east wing for a school. Floor, glazing and a stove, and a man coming from Alresford on the twentieth to measure it. And the curate is to marry Miss Vane. No. The curate is to marry Miss Vane, and the estate is to fit the east wing.”

She settled at last on giving them together, which pleased nobody, since a parish likes to be astonished twice.

Mrs Pike had no such difficulty. She had the whole of it arranged in her own account before the coach had gone out again.

**Mrs Pike** “Where do you suppose Miss Marchmont heard of that cracked beam at all? At my dinner. And where do you suppose Mr Rowe first sat down in a room with Miss Vane and could not think of a word to say to her? At my dinner. A schoolroom and a wedding, and not one of you would believe me.” said Mrs Pike, on her own doorstep, to the sexton. Nobody troubled to correct her. Judith had in fact said it on the step, waiting for the lantern, and the step belonged to Mrs Pike.

Miss Marchmont went up on the Friday coach with her boxes and a roll of measurements. She had got her uncle's promise in writing, and she had got it in a fortnight, and she was not modest about either.

**Miss Marchmont** “Write to me. Not about the weather, and not about who is engaged, because I shall know. Write and tell me whether the stove smokes, because his man swears it will not and I do not believe him.” said Miss Marchmont, from the step of the coach.

**Miss Vane** “I will. And here is something to be going on with.”

Judith gave her the list of six books made out for Mrs Cobbe in October, copied fair, with the shelf numbers against them.

**Miss Marchmont** “Is the orphan in it?”

**Miss Vane** “She is not. I have some standards.”

Mrs Hale watched the coach out and reported that Miss Marchmont had read the whole way to the turn without once looking up, which was thought very odd of her.

Mr Rowe came to the apothecary's on the Saturday and was made to stand on the shop side of the counter, among the drawers of chalk and rhubarb.

**Mr Vane** “You may have her, and I am glad of it, and I shall say so in the Bell so that they hear it from me first. One condition.” said Owen.

**Mr Rowe** “Name it.”

**Mr Vane** “You will never again put my sister into a cart without looking at the wheels of it, and if you cannot tell a sound spoke from a split one, you will come and fetch me, and I will look for you.”

**Mr Rowe** “I cannot tell a sound spoke from a split one.”

**Mr Vane** “I know it. That is why the condition is worded so.”

The banns were read for the first time on the last Sunday in June, and the men were on the east wing roof the same week, stripping the old lead. Fenner came over from Bramdean after all, for the chimney of the stove, and grumbled at being paid out of the estate as though he had not been paid at all.

The subscription book was shut with nine shillings entered in it, and a promise of a load of straw. The straw came. Two of the Barrow girls walked up the lane on the Sunday to look at the east wing with its scaffold, and stood at the gate a full quarter of an hour saying nothing much. Mrs Pike stood on her own step in the sun and accepted the thanks of everybody who passed.

**Mrs Pike** “You are all very welcome. And I am not finished.”
