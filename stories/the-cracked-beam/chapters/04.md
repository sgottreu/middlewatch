## Chapter 4. The Bramdean Road

The scheme was Mrs Pike's. Fenner the mason would not put a figure on paper for anybody who did not come and stand in his yard, Bramdean is six miles, and Mr Rowe had the loan of the Ludds' donkey cart on a Friday.

**Mrs Pike** “Six miles out and six back, and nothing to do the whole way but talk. I have never known it fail.”

On the Friday morning the gatehouse maid came down with a note. Miss Marchmont was at her fourth sheet to her uncle and could not go to Bramdean until the post went out on Tuesday.

Mrs Pike read this on her own doorstep with the cart already at the gate, and Miss Vane came across the green at that moment, going up to the draper's for black thread.

**Mrs Pike** “Miss Vane. You are walking anyway. Go with him and see the figure written down and the man's name under it, for the curate will come home with the sum in his head and forget the shillings.”

**Miss Vane** “I am going for thread.”

**Mrs Pike** “Thread will keep.”

She was still turning the note over when the cart went off up the lane, and it did not occur to her then, or for some hours afterwards, that there was nobody in it but the two of them.

A mile past the mill the near wheel split from the spoke outward, and the cart sat down on its axle. Rowe tied the donkey to the hedge, and there was nothing to do but wait for somebody to come along the road.

They sat on the verge. It was a quarter of an hour before either of them said anything that was not about the wheel.

**Mr Rowe** “It was Chronicles.”

**Miss Vane** “It was.”

**Mr Rowe** “I have carried that morning about with me since November. In my account of it there were six people in the room, and one of the Barrow girls laughed.”

**Miss Vane** “There were four of us and nobody laughed. You had said it twice already and you were going to the visitation on the Thursday. I thought it kinder to say it in front of Mrs Hale than to let you say it in front of the archdeacon.”

**Mr Rowe** “I did not think of it as kindness at the time.”

**Miss Vane** “I have a list of six books that came back into my hand on the green in October. You had not read one of them.”

**Mr Rowe** “I had not. I was ashamed of the word I used before I reached the lych gate, and I have never said so out loud until this minute.”

**Miss Vane** “Trash.”

**Mr Rowe** “Trash. And Mrs Cobbe lay there till February looking at a ceiling.”

Judith looked at the road, which stayed empty. In her own account of that morning he had smiled while he said it. He had not smiled. She had put the smile in herself, somewhere about Christmas, because it made the thing easier to hold.

**Miss Vane** “We have neither of us been telling it straight.”

**Mr Rowe** “No. Mine had grown a good deal in the keeping.”

It was the better part of an hour before Nye came by with a load of hurdles and room for two on the board. He did not hurry his horse for anybody, and the sun was well down before the first cottages showed.

Judith was laughing as they came in, at something he had said about Fenner's yard dog, and she was still laughing when the cart came round the corner of the green.

The green was not empty. It was Friday. The London coach stood outside the Bell with its lamps lit and half the parish about it, and above the draper's the library window was bright, and Mrs Hale was standing in it.
