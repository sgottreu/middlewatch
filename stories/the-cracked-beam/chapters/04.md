## Chapter 4. The Bramdean Road

Miss Marchmont had asked, on the step with the lantern between them, how many children came to the schoolroom.

**Miss Vane** “Twenty-two now. Nine more from the mill cottages, if the lane were passable in February.” said Judith.

**Miss Marchmont** “Twenty-two. And a roof that wants fifteen pounds and has nine shillings.”

**Miss Vane** “Mr Rowe stands under the crack when he reads to them, so that whatever comes away comes away on him.”

Miss Marchmont took the lantern out of the maid's hand and went off across the glebe. She began her letter to her uncle that night. By Friday she had not finished it.

Mrs Pike knew nothing of the letter. Her own arrangement was made. Fenner the mason would put no figure on paper for a man who had not stood in his yard, and Bramdean is six miles, so Rowe had the loan of the Ludds' donkey cart on Friday.

**Mrs Pike** “Six miles out and six back, and nothing to do the whole way but talk. I have never known it fail.” said Mrs Pike. On Friday morning the gatehouse maid came down with a note. Miss Marchmont was at her fourth sheet to her uncle and could not go to Bramdean until Tuesday.

Mrs Pike read it on her own doorstep with the cart already at the gate. Miss Vane came across the green at that moment, going up to the draper's for black thread.

**Mrs Pike** “Miss Vane. You are walking anyway. Go with him and see the figure written down and the man's name under it. He will come home with the sum in his head and lose the shillings.”

**Miss Vane** “I am going for thread.” said Judith.

**Mrs Pike** “Thread will keep.”

It did not occur to Mrs Pike, then or for hours afterwards, that there was nobody in the cart but the two of them.

A mile past the mill the near wheel split from the spoke outward, and the cart sat down on its axle. Rowe tied the donkey to the hedge. There was nothing to do but wait.

They sat on the verge. It was a quarter of an hour before either said anything that was not about the wheel.

**Mr Rowe** “It was Chronicles.” said Rowe.

**Miss Vane** “It was.”

**Mr Rowe** “I have carried that morning about with me since January. In my account there were six people in the room, and one of the Barrow girls laughed.”

**Miss Vane** “There were five of us and nobody laughed. You had said it twice already, and the visitation was on the Thursday. Better Mrs Hale should hear it than the archdeacon.”

**Mr Rowe** “I did not take it for kindness at the time.”

Judith pulled a stem of grass through her fingers.

**Miss Vane** “I wrote out a list of six books in February. It came back into my hand on the green, and you had not read one of them.”

**Mr Rowe** “I had not. I was ashamed of the word I used before I reached the lych gate, and I have never said so aloud until this minute.”

**Miss Vane** “Trash.”

**Mr Rowe** “Trash. And Nan Corbin lay there till the summer with nothing to look at but a ceiling.”

Judith looked at the road, which stayed empty. In her own account he had smiled while he said it. He had not smiled. She had put the smile in herself.

**Miss Vane** “We have neither of us been telling it straight.” she said.

**Mr Rowe** “No. Mine had grown a good deal in the keeping.”

It was the better part of an hour before Nye came by with a load of hurdles and room on the board for two. The sun was well down before the first cottages showed.

Judith was laughing at something Rowe had said about Fenner's yard dog when they came round the corner of the green.

The green was not empty. It was Friday. The London coach stood outside the Bell with its lamps lit and half the parish about it. Above the draper's the library window was bright, and Mrs Hale stood in it.
