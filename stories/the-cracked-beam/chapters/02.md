## Chapter 2. Old Grievances

Mr Rowe had not forgiven Miss Vane for the business of the verse. It had happened in the library in January. He had been giving Mrs Hale the substance of Sunday's sermon, warmly, at some length, and had quoted his text with one word wrong. Miss Vane was at the shelf with her back to him. She said the right words without turning round, in the tone of a woman reading a shop bill, and went on with what she was doing.

Two customers heard her. Mrs Hale repeated the whole of it for a fortnight, always kindly, which was worse. That Miss Vane had been right was not the point. Nobody had asked her, and a man who has been at his books since six may be allowed one word.

Miss Vane had her own account, and hers was of February. Old Nan Corbin at the mill cottages had been laid up with her hip and could not sew or stand, and Rowe went to her every Thursday and read her improving matter until she fell asleep out of self-defence. Judith wrote out a list of six novels the library had, in a fair hand, and gave it to him at the counter.

**Mr Rowe** “I do not think circulating trash is what she wants.”

**Miss Vane** “Have you read any of them?”

He had not, and said so, and thought no more about it. She had kept the list in the ledger drawer for eleven weeks before she burnt it.

So matters stood on the Thursday when Mrs Pike came to the apothecary's back parlour, sat down without being asked twice, and got to her subject before her bonnet strings were undone.

**Mrs Pike** “Now, my dear, you are the only practical creature in this parish. It is to be Wednesday, and it must be a dinner, for you cannot introduce anybody over tea. I shall have Miss Marchmont, and Mr Rowe, and your brother if he will leave off his powders, and eight is a reasonable number for my table. You will not mind the trouble. You never do mind trouble.”

**Miss Vane** “You wish me to order your dinner for you.”

**Mrs Pike** “I wish you to save it. My Hannah cannot be trusted with a joint and a sauce in the same afternoon. And you shall see the young lady before anybody else in Amberlight, which is worth something.”

Judith said she would. She told herself it was for the want of anything else to do between Thursday and Wednesday. It was also because she wished to see for herself what a Marchmont looked like, since the family had held the living since her grandfather's time and had never once been in it.

What she had not counted on was Mr Rowe. Mrs Pike sent him to her twice on Friday and once on Monday, with questions she could have answered herself in half the time.

**Mr Rowe** “Mrs Pike asks whether there are flowers to be had. She particularly said white.”

**Miss Vane** “There is guelder rose in the lane past the smithy. It will be over by Wednesday, but it will be white until then.”

**Mr Rowe** “Thank you. That is very exact.”

**Miss Vane** “You may tell her the ham is spoken for and the pigeons are not. She will want to know which before she asks me herself.”

They were perfectly civil. Every sentence was correct and none of them was meant, and each of them went away certain that the other had been the more disagreeable of the two.

On the Monday evening Owen Vane came through from the dispensary with his sleeves still turned up, and found his sister writing out a list of what Mrs Pike's larder wanted.

**Mr Vane** “That is the third paper you have covered for that woman's table.”

**Miss Vane** “It is a large table.”

**Mr Vane** “It is a small parish. You have been very taken up with the curate's dinner, Judith, for a woman who cannot abide the curate.”

She looked at the paper, and then at the window, and did not find anything to say to him.
