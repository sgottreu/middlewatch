## Chapter 6. The Apothecary's Question

Mr Rowe had two rooms over Mrs Copp's, with a window that looked at the churchyard wall and nothing else. The subscription book lay open on the table.

Owen Vane came up the stairs at four o'clock with his apron still on under his coat, and would not sit.

**Mr Vane** “My sister's name was in three kitchens before dinner. By Sunday it will be in the rest of them. I am not asking whether anything happened on the Bramdean road. I know what a broken wheel is; I have mended two. I am asking what you mean to do.”

**Mr Rowe** “I do not know.”

**Mr Vane** “That is a poor answer to bring a man who shut his shop at half past three.”

**Mr Rowe** “It is. I will give you a better one, and you will like it less. I have known your sister four years and disliked her for one of them, and on Friday I found that I had built the dislike myself out of one morning in November. I know only that whatever is under it came on slowly, and that it is better than what they are saying at the Bell, and that I have no right to hurry it because Mrs Hale stood at a window.”

Owen looked at him for a while.

**Mr Vane** “That is more than I came expecting.”

There were boots on the stair then, coming up two at a time, which no woman in Amberlight did but one. Miss Marchmont had a letter open in her hand.

**Miss Marchmont** “My uncle. His groom brought it over from Winchester within the hour. He will not sell, and he will not pull anything down, and he has agreed to have the old east wing at the house fitted for a school at the estate's charge. Floor, glazing, a stove. His man comes from Alresford on the twentieth to measure it.”

**Mr Rowe** “The east wing has eleven windows.”

**Miss Marchmont** “It has. It will hold forty children and their coats, and it is dry, which is more than can be said for what you have. He asks whether the parish will have it.”

**Mr Rowe** “The parish will have it.”

She was gone down the stairs again before he had thanked her, saying she had four more letters and a man coming about the lead.

Rowe stood with his hand flat on the subscription book. Fifteen pounds. He had carried the figure into Hollis's yard and Mrs Yardley's parlour and up to Mrs Pike's table in a brushed coat and a new neckcloth, and it had been the whole reason he had been got up and presented to anybody. It was not wanted now.

What remained, when the fifteen pounds was taken out of it, was Judith Vane on the verge of the Bramdean road, telling him he had said it twice already and she had thought it kinder than the archdeacon.

**Mr Vane** “You have gone a very strange colour.”

**Mr Rowe** “I have no useful reason left to marry anybody.”

**Mr Vane** “Then you had better say the rest of that to her, and not to me.”

He met her at the turn by the lych gate, coming up, with the look of a woman who has spent an hour deciding something and does not mean to be talked out of it in the road.

**Miss Vane** “I was coming to you.”

**Mr Rowe** “Your brother has been with me this half hour.”

**Miss Vane** “I know. That is why I did not come sooner, and it is not what I have come about. Whatever he has asked you, I am not a thing to be settled between the two of you over a table. I have come to settle it myself.”
