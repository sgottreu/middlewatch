## Chapter 3. Playing the Part

On Tuesday Mrs Pike came into the vestry with the banns already settled in her own mind, and found Mr Rowe writing up a burial.

**Mrs Pike** “Three Sundays, beginning with the next. Miss Selby and Mr Dene. You need not trouble yourself over the particulars, for I have them all.”

Mr Rowe put down the pen.

**Mr Rowe** “I shall read banns when Mr Dene and Miss Selby come to me and ask me for them. That is the whole of my practice and I have never varied it.”

**Mrs Pike** “They are shy of you.”

**Mr Rowe** “Then they may be shy of me in this room. I do not publish a marriage at the word of a third person, however kindly the word is meant.”

He said it with perfect civility, and Mrs Pike went down the path observing to herself that eighty pounds a year had never made a man so grand before.

* * *

By Thursday Mrs Pike had the week arranged for them. There was the walk to Nine Acres on Wednesday, and boiled fowl at her own table on Thursday. On Sunday Margaret was to take Mr Dene's arm along the church path, where the whole green could see it done.

On the Nine Acres road Dene talked about the Ludd boy, who could do long division in his head and could not be got to hold a pen above a minute together. Margaret found she had asked three questions, and had wanted the answers to all three.

* * *

On Friday she came up to the schoolroom with the singing book under her arm. The window was propped open with a slate, and Dene was inside with the schoolmaster from Bramdean, who had walked over about a globe.

**Mr Dene** “The moment her aunt is on the coach I shall put an end to it. It has gone a great deal further than I ever meant it to go, and I have no business in it at all. A man on forty pounds a year has no business in it.”

Margaret stood on the gravel with the book against her. So it had gone further than he meant, and what had gone further was his liking for her; and he would have it back again the moment civility no longer required it of him. She laid the book on the sill and did not go in.

He came out at the sound and caught her at the gate.

**Mr Dene** “You are early. Will you come in and hear them? They have the round in three parts now, after a fashion.”

**Miss Selby** “Thank you, no. I shall be ready at four, as Mrs Pike arranged.”

Her hands were folded. Dene had learnt in a fortnight what that meant, and had not yet learnt what to do about it.

**Mr Dene** “Miss Selby. Have I said something?”

But Mrs Pike was already coming up the lane with Mrs Ferris beside her. Margaret turned and wished them both good morning. Mrs Ferris looked at her niece over the top of her spectacles, and then rather longer at Mr Dene.

**Mrs Pike** “Nerves. It is always nerves, so near the banns.”

**Mrs Ferris** “That is not nerves, ma'am. That is a young woman who has heard something she was not meant to hear, and I should very much like to know what it was.”
