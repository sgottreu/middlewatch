## Chapter 4. Found Out

Miss Judith Vane came to Mrs Copp's on Friday morning with nothing in her hands and no errand from the shop, which was unlike her.

**Miss Vane** “I heard six words in your passage a fortnight ago. Until she goes, and then let it lapse. I have told nobody, and I shall not.”

Margaret folded her hands and said nothing at all.

**Miss Vane** “I came because Mrs Pike has been telling the green all week that you and Mr Dene have quarrelled.”

**Miss Selby** “He told the Bramdean master he would put an end to it the day my aunt takes the coach.”

**Miss Vane** “Yes. To the pretence.”

Margaret looked up.

**Miss Vane** “He said he had had the good of it and never earned a shilling of it. A man does not speak of a woman's regard as a debt unless he minds very much about the owing.”

There was a silence in which the kitchen clock was extremely audible.

**Miss Selby** “You might have left me in it.”

**Miss Vane** “I dislike a story that does not add up, and this one has not added up since Monday the ninth.”

She went down the green towards her brother's shop and told nobody there either.

* * *

Margaret reached the schoolroom before eleven, with her bonnet strings loose. Dene was alone at the long desk, ruling a line in the coal account.

**Miss Selby** “What did you mean to put an end to?”

**Mr Dene** “The lie. What else was there?”

**Miss Selby** “I thought you meant me.”

He put the pen down and came round the desk.

**Mr Dene** “I meant to say it to you honestly, once, with nobody arranging us. I have been eleven days trying to find a quarter of an hour when Mrs Pike was not at the window.”

He had her hand by then, and she had let him take it. Neither of them heard the latch.

**Mrs Pike** “There! And they said they had quarrelled.”

Mrs Ferris came in past her and set her gloves down on the nearest desk.

**Mrs Ferris** “Mr Dene. My niece has eighteen pounds a year and a turned gown. If I settle not one shilling on her, would you marry her?”

**Mr Dene** “Yes.”

**Mrs Ferris** “On forty pounds a year.”

**Mr Dene** “On forty pounds a year, in two rooms, with the coal account unpaid. I would rather have her penniless and honestly mine than take her with your money and spend my life knowing which of us had bought the marriage. I am poor. I am not for sale.”

Mrs Ferris looked at him over the top of her spectacles. She had put that question once before, to a young man in Bath in the year nine, over eight hundred pounds, and he had taken until the morning to answer it.

**Mrs Ferris** “Well. Something has happened here after all.”

**Mrs Pike** “Of course something has happened. I have been saying so since Easter.”
