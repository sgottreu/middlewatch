## Chapter 2. The Convenient Fiction

On Monday morning Mr Robert Dene came to Mrs Copp's door with his hat already in his hand, and asked for Miss Selby before he was well over the step.

**Mr Dene** “I have had the speech by heart since four o'clock. There was no engagement, Mrs Pike was mistaken, and I am very sorry she was. Four sentences. I shall say it to her this morning and be at the schoolroom by nine.”

Margaret folded her hands.

**Miss Selby** “And what does my aunt hear, when she hears it?”

**Mr Dene** “The truth.”

**Miss Selby** “She hears that I have eighteen pounds a year, no prospect, and nobody in the parish who wants me. She will have my boxes corded by Thursday and I shall read the Bath newspaper aloud for thirty years. Say your four sentences, Mr Dene, and you will have told her exactly what she came to find out.”

Dene turned his hat round once and set it on the table.

**Mr Dene** “A fortnight, she said.”

**Miss Selby** “A fortnight. Then she goes back to Bath, and by Christmas people will say I thought better of it, and by Lady Day they will have found something else to say. Nothing is asked of you but to be civil to me in public, which you have managed for two years without difficulty.”

**Mr Dene** “It was easier when it was not required of me.”

In the passage outside, Miss Judith Vane had come to the door with a draught from her brother's shop for old Nan Corbin, and found Mr Rowe, the curate, on the step before her with the same errand in his mouth.

**Mr Rowe** “Miss Vane. I was told at the shop that nothing had been sent up, and as I lodge here, and as the matter was pressing, it seemed to me that the shortest way”

**Miss Vane** “It was sent up. I am the sending.”

She put the bottle into his hand. He thanked her with great correctness, and she wished him a good morning with as much, and each went away thinking the other had a very high opinion of themselves for eighty pounds a year and a brother's back shop respectively.

But Judith had stood in the passage the length of six words, and the six words were these: until she goes, and then let it lapse. She walked home along the green doing the arithmetic. Mr Dene had not spoken to Owen for a licence, and a man on forty pounds a year who means to marry speaks to somebody about money first. She said nothing to anybody, which was her habit.

At eleven, Mrs Ferris came up from the Bell and placed her gloves on her knee.

**Mrs Ferris** “I am told the schoolroom is the gentleman's whole fortune. Then I shall see it. Wednesday, at the hour the children are in it. I have never yet known what a man was worth from being told it in a parlour.”

Dene opened his mouth, and shut it, and bowed.
