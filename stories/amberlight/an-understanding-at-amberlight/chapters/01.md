## Chapter 1. A Visitor from Bath

Mrs Augusta Ferris came off the Friday coach at the Bell with two trunks and a maid, and was in her niece's lodging within the quarter hour. She had two thousand a year from a Bath wine merchant, dead these six years, and she looked round Mrs Copp's front parlour over the top of her spectacles as though pricing the carpet.

**Mrs Ferris** “Four shillings a week, I suppose. And you teach the scales to farmers' daughters.”

Margaret Selby folded her hands.

**Miss Selby** “Six of them. Eighteen pounds a year, when the Hollis girls pay at Michaelmas.”

Her aunt sat down without being asked and arranged her gloves on her knee.

**Mrs Ferris** “That is not a future, Margaret. That is a way of postponing one. You will come back to Bath with me. You shall have a chair by my fire, and read to me, and want for nothing.”

**Miss Selby** “As a companion.”

**Mrs Ferris** “As a niece who is paid in her dinner, which is the same article under a politer name. I am fifty-four and I have given up pretending about such things.”

Margaret said nothing. It was a thing she did very well, and her aunt was not deceived by it for a moment.

* * *

On Sunday the church was full, and the congregation came out slowly into a grey wind. Margaret stopped in the porch to speak to Mr Robert Dene, who kept the old parish schoolroom on forty pounds a year.

**Mr Dene** “The coal account stands at two pounds eleven, and the vestry will not look at it before November.”

**Miss Selby** “Then I shall take no payment for the singing until it is settled. We had an understanding on that point, Mr Dene, and I mean to keep to it.”

**Mr Dene** “We had. I did not look for you to hold to it so exactly.”

Mrs Honoria Pike had come up behind them with her prayer book pressed to her bombazine, and she heard the one word she had been waiting eleven months to hear.

**Mrs Pike** “My dear Miss Selby, I will not pretend I did not hear it, and I will not pretend I am sorry. An understanding! With Mr Dene! I have expected it since Easter.”

Half the parish had stopped on the path. Mrs Ferris looked at Mr Dene over the top of her spectacles, and then at her niece.

**Mrs Ferris** “Engaged. Well. That alters the question entirely.”

**Mrs Pike** “You are the aunt from Bath. I knew it by the silk.”

**Mrs Ferris** “I am. And I shall stay a fortnight at the Bell and see my niece properly courted before anybody says another word to me about Bath. I am not against a poor marriage, ma'am. I am against a convenient one.”

Mr Dene stood with his hat in his hand and did not speak, and Mrs Pike took his silence for modesty. He had come to church owing four shillings for glazing. He went down the path engaged, and he had not said one word in it.
