Make this the story in which Miss Judith Vane and Mr Silas Rowe are married. They became engaged in June 1817, their banns were read at the end of that month, and they marry no one else; in this story they are engaged from the first page, and it ends with their wedding at Amberlight church.

Keep exactly: the title; the setting ("Amberlight, Hampshire, autumn 1817"); the tropes, exactly as named; the guest cast (Mrs Winifred Cardew, Miss Flora Easton, Mr Aubrey Stanhope) with their names, ages and stations; Mr Owen Vane; the west-wing gothic, Flora's whispered "the prayer", the false-backed cabinet, and the marriage lines hidden in Sir Edward's prayer book in his pew.

Replace: the logline and the promise (no proposal to be refused; a bribe refused and a scandal that ends at the altar), and the chapters and their beats. Eight chapters, 4 to 5 concrete beats each — things that happen, not moods.

Character rules:
- Judith and Rowe are engaged throughout. No proposal from anyone to Judith, no rival suitor taken seriously, no estrangement between them. Rowe does not propose; he is already accepted.
- Mrs Pike never promotes another man for Judith; she takes credit for this engagement and would never undo her own work. If she talks to Stanhope about Judith, it is to boast of the coming wedding, which tells him exactly what Judith has to lose.
- Stanhope's offer to Judith is money, not marriage: a comfortable sum toward a curate's household if she will tell him what she finds in the west wing before Mrs Cardew does. She refuses.
- Put Mrs Hale, the draper's wife who keeps the library counter, in the cast: the gossip in chapter 2 and chapter 6 runs through her window and her counter.

Setting rules:
- How Judith learns the inheritance question: from a letter from Miss Louisa Marchmont, her correspondent, who writes that her uncle acts as Sir Edward's executor and that the house waits on a question of a marriage. Not from the parish register (it holds baptisms, marriages and burials, not ownership) and not from "old parish papers".
- Louisa's uncle authorised the east-wing schoolroom that June as executor, so his authority must stand. Flora's proved marriage lines make her Sir Edward's heir, but she is under age: the house stays in the executor's keeping until she comes of age. At the end she and Mrs Cardew leave for Bath for the winter, and the west wing's shutters are opened. Nobody takes possession of the main rooms.
- Whoever sees Judith and Rowe leave the dark house late at night is Sarah Copp, Mrs Copp's daughter, who repeats things — not Mrs Pike, who would not be out walking at midnight. They do not go for the Bramdean smith in the night; Bramdean is six miles off. They wait for the village smith at first light.
- The scandal threatens the wedding, not the engagement: a churchwarden asks Rowe to put the wedding off until the talk dies down, and Owen is made uncomfortable before his own customers.
- Do not say where the Rowes will live afterwards, and do not mention a curate's cottage.

Ending: the marriage lines are found in Sir Edward's pew; Stanhope's claim collapses before two proofs; Mrs Cardew gives the west library's shelves to the schoolroom; Mrs Pike plans a wedding breakfast nobody asked her to arrange; the last chapter ends at the wedding itself, in the same church, with Judith walking out as Mrs Rowe.
