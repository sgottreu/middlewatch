## Summary

None of the six is ready to write as it stands. All six outlines were made on 29 August, before *The Cracked Beam* (summer 1817) and *The Oakmere Will* (October 1816) set the canon they now break.

The biggest problem is Judith. Five of the six make her the romantic lead, and four end with her engaged or proposed to. The bible says she and Rowe first become engaged in June 1817 and that she marries no one else. *The Cracked Beam* also shows they disliked each other until that summer.

The second problem is repetition. Over one autumn in 1816, a parish of four hundred gets three will mysteries, all solved with the apothecary's records. Across the slate, three stories have a secret benefactor whose credit goes to Mrs Pike, and two have a gothic plot about lights in Amberlight House.

## Series-level problems

These issues cut across several outlines, so it's cheaper to fix them once in the ideator brief than six times by hand.

**1. Judith is everyone's love interest.** Canon now fixes her romance. Before June 1817 she and Rowe are prickly: he resents her correcting his sermon aloud last winter, and she resents him waving off her novels. That mutual misjudgment is *The Cracked Beam*'s whole engine, and they become engaged in June 1817. She marries Rowe and no one else.

- A story set before summer 1817 can use the friction, but not an earlier proposal, refusal, understanding or warmth that resolves it.
- A story set after it can't use estrangement, rival suitors who are taken seriously, or another proposal.

**2. The same mystery keeps coming back.**

| Device | Oakmere (finished, Oct 1816) | Fenwick (1816) | Bell (1816) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Disputed will or codicil | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Apothecary's records prove the testator's state | Dispensing book | Visit ledger | Prescription signatures |
| Housekeeper or maid as the key witness | Mrs Whitlow | Sally Prewitt | Mrs Thorne |
| Indebted cousin or nephew as villain | Hartwell | Ashby | Peter Hale |
| Misdirected or forgotten letter | Yes | Yes | Yes |

A viewer who watches two of these will see the solution coming. Owen's records should solve one mystery in the series, and it has already been used.

**3. Three secret benefactors, with Mrs Pike wrongly credited each time.** *The Thatcher's Bill*, *The Letter at the Bell* and *The Lit Windows* each have an anonymous payment the parish credits to the wrong person, usually Mrs Pike. Keep this in one story at most.

**4. Two gothic stories about Amberlight House.** *The Lit Windows* and *The Shuttered Wing* both open with lit windows in the empty house. Both hide a grieving young woman who is an heir of Sir Edward, and both use a chapter titled "Lights at Amberlight". Only one should get the house.

**5. Cast copied wrong from the bible.**

- *An Understanding* and *The Thatcher's Bill* call the apothecary **Mr Edmund Vane** with no voice. The bible has **Mr Owen Vane** (Archer), and *The Oakmere Will* has already used Owen.
- *The Thatcher's Bill* lists **"Silas Rowe"** without "Mr", and with no recurring flag or voice.
- Every story set before 1817 uses the anchor-year ages unchanged. *The Oakmere Will* subtracted correctly, giving Judith 26, Rowe 33 and Pike 57 in 1816. The bible requires that.
- **Mrs Hale is in none of the six**, though the bible calls her the parish's real information system. Gossip keeps running through Mrs Pike alone.

**6. Old voice pins.** Every unwritten manifest still pins `genre_voices` to Alice, Lily, Matilda, Daniel, George and Brian. Those are the retired voices from before the November recasting. This isn't a story problem, but fix it before the record stage.

## An Understanding at Amberlight

Romance · autumn 1815 · 15 min · ideate approved. Verdict: **rework.** The whole plot is a courtship between Rowe and Judith that ends with banns read in 1815, and canon puts their first banns on the last Sunday of June 1817.

**Character issues**

- Judith and Rowe fall for each other and have their banns read, which contradicts both the June 1817 engagement and the dislike *The Cracked Beam* shows.
- After a public false engagement, *The Cracked Beam* would have to mention it. It doesn't, and Mrs Pike is shocked there that Judith is the one being talked about.
- The apothecary is called "Mr Edmund Vane" with "a struggling shop". He should be Owen: dry and blunt, and he would never be pleased to see his sister used to settle his accounts.
- Ages are anchor-year values. In 1815 Rowe should be 32, Judith 25, Pike 56 and Owen 28.
- Mrs Ferris settles money on Judith, but every later story and the bible say Judith has "no fortune of her own". That gift can't happen.
- Mrs Ferris is Judith's aunt by marriage and has never been in the bible. If she's kept, she's a new relation who has to stay in canon.

**Setting issues**

- Mrs Pike, a widow of 58, congratulates people "at the Bell that evening before half the parish". An inn's public room at night is a stretch for her. A parish tea or the church porch after service fits better.
- No other setting conflicts. The coach days and distances aren't touched.

**Revision ideas**

1. **Recommended: give the false engagement to Owen.** Mrs Pike mishears and announces Owen Vane engaged to a guest woman, perhaps a farmer's widowed daughter or the new schoolmistress. That stops a rich aunt carrying off the guest, not Judith. Owen's bluntness makes a better fake-engagement comedy than Rowe's earnestness. Judith keeps her bible role as the one who untangles what Pike said. The trope, title and promise all survive.
2. **Two guest leads.** Keep the plot whole but cast two new young people. Rowe stays in a supporting role as the curate who must read banns he suspects are false. That's an honest comic dilemma for a man who is good at the work.
3. **Move to spring 1817 and make it a failure.** Pike's fake engagement of Rowe and Judith collapses in mutual irritation, and the story ends with them further apart. It works only if *The Cracked Beam*'s grievances are rewritten to include it, so this isn't recommended.

*Later decision (2026-11-11): option 2 was used. Owen can't be engaged in 1815, because canon has Judith keeping house for him, unmarried, through 1817.*

## The Letter at Fenwick Cottage

Mystery · early autumn 1816 · 15 min · ideate approved. Verdict: **rework.** It invents a past between Rowe and Judith that canon doesn't have and ends with them engaged in 1816. It also repeats *The Oakmere Will*'s solution in the same autumn.

**Character issues**

- The logline calls Judith "the woman who once refused him", and chapter 3 has business "unspoken for seven years". *The Cracked Beam* establishes no earlier proposal, only last winter's petty grievances.
- In chapter 5 Rowe proposes and Judith "gives her word" in 1816, about nine months before canon's engagement.
- In 1816 Judith and Rowe already appear in *The Oakmere Will* as easy colleagues on parish business, with no romantic charge. Fenwick happens in the same few weeks and would need to agree with that.
- "He no longer needs her fortune, only her word" makes no sense, because she has no fortune in any version.
- Ages should be Rowe 33, Judith 26 and Pike 57.
- The apothecary is unnamed and plays only as a ledger. If he appears, he should be Owen with his voice, Archer.

**Setting issues**

- Early autumn 1816 is a few weeks before *The Oakmere Will* (October 1816). Both are disputed wills, both turn on the apothecary's record of the dying person's state, and both have Mrs Pike broadcasting the opening news.
- "Fenwick cottage" and the late Mrs Fenwick aren't on the map yet. That's fine, but they become canon once this is written.
- Walking "past the empty gates of Amberlight House" in 1816 is correct.
- The Friday and Tuesday coaches are used correctly.

**Revision ideas**

1. **Recommended: make it a Mrs Rowe story, set in 1818 or later.** A married couple investigating together is a dynamic the series doesn't have yet, and a curate's wife can call at a house of mourning beside him without remark. Drop the second-chance trope for *The meddling matchmaker* or a new one. Give the romance to Eleanor Fenwick, with a better man than Ashby or a redeemed Ashby, as the outline already half-suggests.
2. **Keep 1816 but change the evidence.** Take the apothecary's ledger out. Solve it with Sally's timing, Yarrow's letter, and the carrier's misdirection, which was the story's own hook. Rowe and Judith stay colleagues, with no lychgate scene and no proposal.
3. **Merge it with *The Letter at the Bell*.** Two misdirected-letter will mysteries in one autumn is one too many. Fold Fenwick's best idea, Mrs Pike reading someone else's legal letter to half the green, into a single stronger will story and free a slot for a different flavor.

*Later decision (2026-11-11): options 1 and 3 combined. See "Merge plan" below.*

## The Thatcher's Bill

Comedy of manners · "some three years after 1812", so about autumn 1815 · 15 min · ideate approved. Verdict: **revise.** The comedy holds up. Its problem is that it runs *The Cracked Beam*'s arc early, where Rowe and Judith misjudge each other as proud, then talk honestly and reach a settled beginning.

**Character issues**

- The ending, "a small settled beginning between them", resolves the misjudgment two years before *The Cracked Beam* needs it intact.
- Even the grievance device repeats: a curt note returning a borrowed book here, and novels dismissed in *The Cracked Beam*.
- Rowe is listed as "Silas Rowe", recurring is empty and voice is empty. Copy the bible entry verbatim: Mr Silas Rowe, Sam.
- The apothecary is called "Mr Edmund Vane" again. He should be Owen.
- Judith secretly pays the debt from "her own small savings" through a Winchester solicitor. That's a big gesture for someone with no fortune, and doing it through a solicitor is overbuilt for a thatcher's bill.
- "Mr Aldous Finch" reuses the first name of Aldous Fenner from *The Oakmere Will*. Rename him.
- Ages should be Rowe 32, Judith 25 and Pike 56.

**Setting issues**

- "The parsonage roof": the bible houses Rowe in two rooms over Mrs Copp's. A curate on eighty a year doesn't hold the parsonage, and its repairs fall to the rector or the patron.
- Finch is a Marchmont cousin "considering his interest in the living and the empty house". Canon says the Marchmont family never came into the parish before summer 1817. A cousin who isn't strictly family is a grey area, and it weakens Louisa's arrival as the first visit.
- "Respect for his work with the parish school" is consistent, since the old schoolroom exists before 1817.
- The Tuesday coach guard and the Friday coach out are both consistent.

**Revision ideas**

1. **Recommended: keep 1815 and end it in dramatic irony.** Judith quietly pays the debt through Owen and Rowe never finds out. The guest is exposed, but the misjudgment between Rowe and Judith survives, so the viewer knows something *The Cracked Beam* will pay off. Drop the final book-return beat or turn it sour.
2. **Swap the benefactor.** Owen pays the bill, true to his "shuts the shop if something matters" note. Judith is the one who works it out. Rowe and Judith stay at arm's length, and the secret-benefactor trope moves away from Judith entirely.
3. **Set it after 1817 as a household comedy.** The Rowes' first winter is a thatcher's bill they can't pay, and someone in the parish pays it. This needs Finch replaced, because there's no courtship left for him to threaten.

*Later decision (2026-11-11): option 1, with the debt moved to the old schoolroom's re-thatching and the trope* The misdirected letter *swapped for* The unwelcome proposal.

## The Letter at the Bell

Mystery · autumn 1816 · 30 min · ideate approved. Verdict: **rework.** Judith ends it in an understanding with a solicitor's clerk, which the bible rules out, and two recurring characters act against their bible notes. Its `bible_conflict` field holds the string `"omit"` rather than `null`, so the ideator's check didn't run properly.

**Character issues**

- Edward Sorrel is cast as Judith's love interest. In the last beat he asks to settle in Amberlight, and she says "it might suit them both". She marries Rowe and no one else, and nine months later she's engaged to him with no Sorrel in sight.
- Rowe forgets a dying parishioner's sealed will in the vestry for weeks. The bible calls him "genuinely good at the work and hopeless at everything adjacent to it". This failure is the work itself, so it breaks his core trait.
- Mrs Pike delivers coin-filled baskets for weeks, accepts thanks, and "never thought to say" they weren't from her. That inverts her bible note, which says she knows everything first and tells all of it. It's also odd for Rowe to trust her with a secret.
- The logline calls Judith "the quiet woman who reads everyone else's letters before they do". That's closer to Mrs Hale. The bible's Judith is the one person who checks a story before repeating it.
- "Old Mr Josiah Hale", "Mr Peter Hale" and "Hale's Bottom" collide with **Mrs Hale**, the draper's wife and a recurring character. Viewers will assume they're related.
- The apothecary is unnamed again and supplies prescription notes with Hale's signature. That's the third use of the apothecary's records to prove a will.
- Ages should be Judith 26, Rowe 33 and Pike 57.

**Setting issues**

- Autumn 1816 is the same season as *The Oakmere Will* and Fenwick: three dead landholders with disputed estates in one autumn, in a parish of four hundred.
- Hale's Bottom, worth about a hundred and fifty a year, adds a third farm next to Oakmere and Upper Barton, with no direction or distance given. Place it on the map before writing.
- The vestry and Sunday-service proposal scenes are fine. The coach days in the closing line are correct.

**Revision ideas**

1. **Recommended: give the romance to Sorrel and Constance.** The clerk falls for the ward whose case he's sent to settle, which is a real conflict of interest with his firm. Judith stays the investigator and ends unattached, so the bible stays clean and the second half gets a better engine.
2. **Rename the Hale family** to something like Pell of Pell's Bottom. **Fix Rowe's lapse:** Hale swore him to hold the letter unopened until Mrs Thorne came for it, and she never came. That makes his silence a fault of keeping a promise, not of forgetting it.
3. **Hand the baskets to Mrs Hale, or drop them.** If the benefactor plot stays, the person delivering should be someone who would actually keep quiet, or Mrs Pike should loudly and wrongly name a donor. Better, drop the secret-benefactor thread, which *The Thatcher's Bill* and *The Lit Windows* also use.
4. **Move it to spring 1818 or later** so it doesn't share an autumn with Oakmere. Prove the will with Mrs Thorne's seal and Hale's handwriting in the farm's own account books rather than the apothecary's.

## Merge plan: Fenwick into The Letter at the Bell

Merge the two into one 30-minute story, kept in the `the-letter-at-the-bell` bundle and set in spring 1818. Mrs Rowe is the investigator, and the romance goes to the guests. Fenwick contributes the opening hook and the alibi; the Bell contributes the clerk, the public proposal and the letter the curate holds. Owen's records, the secret benefactor and the baskets are cut.

**Why the Bell bundle survives:** a `revise` keeps the length pinned at creation, and only the Bell is 30 minutes. Two mysteries' worth of plot needs eight chapters. Fenwick's 15-minute slot is then free.

### The married-couple engine

The old outline's weak point, Rowe forgetting a will in the vestry, becomes the story's spine. The late Mrs Fenwick gave Mr Rowe a sealed letter under a promise, with a condition: he may open it only if someone claims the cottage against her niece *in writing, to him*. He's good at the work, so he keeps the promise even from his wife.

Mrs Rowe, the one person who checks a story before repeating it, sees he knows something. She doesn't ask him to break his word. She works out the condition and arranges for it to be met. That's a conflict only a married couple can have, and it's true to both bible notes.

### Cut

- Owen's ledger and the prescription-signature handwriting match. *The Oakmere Will* used the apothecary's records.
- Mrs Pike's baskets and the secret benefactor. *The Thatcher's Bill* and *The Lit Windows* already carry that trope.

## The Lit Windows of Amberlight

Gothic · late autumn 1816 · 15 min · ideate awaiting review, with one earlier draft. Verdict: **re-date.** The ideator flagged the main conflict itself in `bible_conflict`: canon says Amberlight House stood empty until summer 1817. The guest cast and character work are otherwise the best on the slate.

**Character issues**

- The guest leads, Eliza Combe and Nathaniel Calder, are new and clean, which is what the revision asked for. No recurring character is pulled out of their bible role.
- "Miss Cecily Marchmont" is a Marchmont living in the parish in 1816. Canon says the family had never once come into the parish before summer 1817, and that Louisa's fortnight at the gatehouse was that first visit.
- "Nathaniel" is also Captain Farr's first name in *The Oakmere Will*. Two Nathaniels in a parish of four hundred is avoidable.
- Ages should be Pike 57 and Rowe 33.
- Mrs Pike is wrongly credited with an anonymous payment, the third time on the slate.

**Setting issues**

- The house is occupied and lit in 1816, which is a direct canon conflict.
- "After ten years empty": Sir Edward died in spring 1812, so it was four and a half years by late 1816. Even moved to 1818 it's six.
- Calder pays "two years' arrears owed [Rowe] from the Marchmont living". A curate is paid by the rector, not the patron. It only works if the living was vacant and the patron was sequestering it. *The Cracked Beam* mentions "a rector three years dead", so this may be canon waiting to be pinned down rather than an error. Decide before writing.
- Eliza keeps "her late mother's dressmaking shop", a second shop beside the draper's. That's plausible, but the draper is the Hales' business, so the two would be rivals. Use that or acknowledge it.

**Revision ideas**

1. **Recommended: move it to late autumn 1818.** The bible already says a story may open any part of the house. Calder takes the main block as trustee while the east-wing schoolroom carries on next door. That gives the gothic a lovely contrast: children's voices by day, a shuttered grief at night. Rename Cecily to a non-Marchmont ward of the estate, or keep her a Marchmont and have Louisa's letter to Mrs Rowe announce her coming.
2. **Keep 1816 and move to another house.** Use a different shut-up property, such as a dower house or a farm left empty after a death. The parish's guessing game doesn't need Amberlight House specifically. This frees the house for *The Shuttered Wing*.
3. **Keep this one and cut The Shuttered Wing's house plot.** If Calder and Eliza get the house, Flora's inheritance mystery needs a different building. Whichever you choose, only one of these two gothic stories should own "lights at Amberlight". Also fix the anonymous payment: Calder's trust money can be traced to Calder by Eliza, without the parish crediting Mrs Pike.

*Later decision (2026-11-10): option 1.*

## The Shuttered Wing

Gothic · autumn 1817 · 30 min · ideate awaiting review, with one earlier draft. Verdict: **revise.** This is the only outline that honors the schoolroom, and the west-wing note landed. But it's set after the June engagement and still treats Judith as unattached, and it ends with Rowe proposing to a woman who accepted him in June.

**Character issues**

- In chapter 8 Rowe asks Judith "whether eighty pounds a year... might do for a life", which is a second proposal. By October 1817 their banns have been read and they may already be married. She would be Mrs Rowe after the wedding.
- Mrs Pike promotes Stanhope as "a very suitable match for a girl with no fortune". Pike takes credit for the engagement in her own bible note, so she would never push a rival.
- Stanhope proposes marriage to Judith to rescue her from scandal. Against an engaged or married woman that's a bribe, not an offer, and the beat has to become one.
- The scandal of the curate and the apothecary's sister alone in the dark house is milder for an engaged couple. It could delay the wedding or embarrass the rector, but it can't threaten the match.
- Rowe goes "turning up old parish papers" and finds Sir Edward's will terms. A will wouldn't be in parish papers. An attested copy sent to the incumbent, or Louisa's letter to Judith, would work.
- Owen and Pike are copied correctly. Mrs Hale is missing, though chapter 2's gossip "at the circulating library" is her scene.

**Setting issues**

- Using the west wing is fine. The bible says a story may open any part of the house, and the bible must be updated when this one finishes.
- Flora as heiress presumptive whose claim depends on proving a marriage has to square with canon. Louisa's uncle authorized the schoolroom "at the estate's charge" that June. Make the uncle the executor or trustee acting until the marriage question is settled.
- "Ownership never settled in the five years" is correct arithmetic. Checking "the old parish register" for ownership is the wrong document, since registers hold baptisms, marriages and burials.
- Mrs Pike out after midnight "walking a sick neighbour's dog" is implausible for a widow of 58. Use Mrs Copp's daughter Sarah, who "repeats things" and is already in the minor names, or the sexton.
- Fetching "the Bramdean smith" at midnight means a 12-mile round trip. Use a village smith, or have them wait until morning.
- Rowe's "curate's cottage" contradicts his two rooms over Mrs Copp's. After the wedding they need somewhere to live, which this story could settle.

**Revision ideas**

1. **Recommended: make this the wedding story.** The bible says the wedding is "not yet written" and a later story may set it. Keep autumn 1817, but Judith and Rowe are engaged throughout, and the scandal threatens a postponed wedding and Rowe's standing with the rector. Stanhope offers money for Judith's help finding the papers, not marriage. The marriage lines are found in Sir Edward's pew and the wedding happens in that same church. Cut the proposal beat.
2. **Keep it gothic and give the romance to Flora.** Introduce a guest suitor whom Stanhope's claim threatens. Judith and Rowe investigate as an engaged pair.
3. **Settle the house question with The Lit Windows.** If this story keeps Amberlight House, *The Lit Windows* moves to another building, or the two become one gothic story.

*Later decision (2026-11-11): option 1. Flora is proved heir but under age, so the house stays with the Marchmont executor; this keeps* The Lit Windows *(1818) consistent.*
