# Not sounding like a machine

Adapted for narrative fiction from Wikipedia's *Signs of AI writing* (CC BY-SA),
which catalogues these patterns for encyclopedic prose. Its own caution applies
doubly here.

## Read this part first

**These are symptoms. The disease is regression to the mean.**

A language model predicts the most probable continuation, so it drifts toward the
statement that would be true of the widest range of subjects. A specific, strange,
particular fact gets smoothed into a generic and slightly inflated one. The
Wikipedia guide's example: "inventor of the first train-coupling device" becomes
"a revolutionary titan of industry." The subject gets simultaneously vaguer and
grander.

In fiction that failure looks like this: a character who should have one odd
specific habit instead has a Feeling, described in the register of a book blurb.
A room that should smell of tallow and wet wool is instead "atmospheric." A
refusal that should be four flat words becomes a paragraph about the difficulty
of the decision.

**So do not just delete the flagged words.** Swapping "crucial" for "vital"
changes nothing. If a sentence trips one of the rules below, the usual repair is
to replace the generality with something particular that only this story could
contain — a sum of money, a garment, a name, a thing someone actually did.
Word-policing alone produces prose that is stilted *and* still generic, which is
worse than what you started with.

**Do not overcorrect.** Formal diction, correct grammar, long sentences, ordinary
transitions, and the occasional semicolon are not tells; period fiction is
supposed to have them. Deliberately clumsy writing is a different failure, not a
fix.

## Hard rules

**No AI vocabulary.** These are overused to the point of being a signature, and
they cluster — where one appears others usually follow. Take the list literally:
the synonyms are fine, and a word can be innocent in its concrete sense
(*underscore* the line, a *vibrant* dye).

> additionally (opening a sentence), align with, boasts (meaning "has"), bolster,
> crucial, deep dive, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, foster, garner,
> highlight (as a verb), interplay, intricate, intricacies, key (as an adjective),
> landscape (as an abstraction), meticulous, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry,
> testament, underscore (as a verb), valuable, vibrant

Most are anachronistic in Regency narration anyway, which makes them cheap to cut.

**No trailing analytic participle.** A clause hung on the end of a sentence that
tells the reader what the sentence meant. This is the single most recognizable
machine construction in existence.

> *She returned the letter unopened, underscoring the finality of her decision.*
> *He took the living at Combe, marking a turn in his fortunes.*

End at the fact. `She returned the letter unopened.` The reader can do the rest.
Ordinary participles are fine — *she sat by the window, sewing* is a real action,
not an interpretation.

**No negative parallelism.** "Not just X, but Y." "It was not X; it was Y." "No X,
no Y, just Z." At most **one** in an entire story, and only where the contrast is
the actual argument of the sentence.

**Plain copulas.** *Was* and *had*, not *served as*, *stood as*, *functioned as*,
*represented*, *marked*, *boasted*, *featured*, *offered*. The Wikipedia guide
notes a measurable collapse in the use of "is" in post-2023 prose. Prose written
before 2023, in any genre, uses plain copulas constantly.

**No manufactured significance.** Nothing "stands as a testament to", "marks a
turning point", "reflects a broader shift", "leaves an indelible mark", or plays
"a crucial role". In fiction this is worse than puffery: it is the narrator
telling the reader how to feel about a scene the narrator has not yet earned. Our
narrator reports and lets the reader judge.

**No promotional register.** *Nestled*, *in the heart of*, *renowned*, *rich*
(as in *rich history*, *rich tapestry*), *profound*, *diverse array*, *natural
beauty*, *groundbreaking*, *exemplifies*, *commitment to*. Travel-brochure prose
about a parsonage is instantly detectable.

**No modern therapeutic register.** Closure, boundaries, processing, toxic,
validate, self-care, unpack, hold space, trauma as a noun about feelings,
"in that moment", "little did she know", "a mixture of X and Y" as an emotion.

**Spaced em dashes.** A dash with spaces on both sides — like this — is the
punched-up sales cadence, not period punctuation. Regency prose uses the dash
freely, but unspaced, and as an interruption rather than as emphasis. Two per
chapter at most.

## Density rules

These are fine once and a tell three times.

**Rule of three.** Adjective triples and three-item lists. The world does not come
in threes as often as a model thinks. Use two or four unless the content genuinely
has three parts.

**Elegant variation.** Models carry a repetition penalty and so rename the same
thing every time it appears: *the letter*, then *the missive*, then *the
correspondence*, then *the written communication*. Real prose repeats the plain
noun. Call the letter a letter.

**Uniform sentence length.** A paragraph where every sentence runs twenty to
thirty words is machine cadence regardless of vocabulary. Vary hard. A long
balanced period, then four words.

The long period is welcome — but *balanced* is the operative word, and this is
heard rather than read. A sentence that coordinates three or more clauses with
commas and conjunctions loses its listener at the third, because the subject of
the first is gone by then. Vary length freely; keep the shape of a sentence
consistent within itself.

**Every paragraph the same shape.** Statement, elaboration, closing summary,
repeated down the page. Let some paragraphs be a single line of dialogue.

## The test that actually matters

Read the chapter and ask: could any of these sentences appear in a story about
different people in a different county? Every sentence that could, should be
rewritten until it could not.
