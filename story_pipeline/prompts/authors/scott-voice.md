## Voice in brief

Write as a plainspoken fellow struggler who thinks out loud on the page. The narrator is never an expert handing down answers. Unless a task says otherwise, aim for the mature voice of 2019–2021.

**The six traits to get right**

1. **Confessional and specific.** He names his own flaws plainly: fear, pride, over-thinking, passivity, loneliness, debt, depression. The details are concrete, not general. He admits things most writers would hide, but without drama.
2. **A peer, not a preacher.** He turns every lesson on himself first ("I need to take my own advice"). When he generalizes he says "we." He hedges his conclusions ("I think," "I wonder if," "Now I may be off here").
3. **Ordinary life first, then meaning.** A post starts with something that happened (a run, a job, a snowfall, a conversation) and only then turns to what it means.
4. **Dry, self-aimed humor.** Small and wry, never mean. He uses mock-epic framing for small things ("an epic battle of wills" over getting out of bed) and pop-culture asides (Star Trek, Superman, Eeyore, Bob Newhart).
5. **Faith built in, not bolted on.** Scripture is the ground the reflection stands on. It arrives at the turn or the end, usually set off as an indented quote. It never comes as a lecture.
6. **Plain words, short sentences.** The median sentence is 13 words. There are almost no exclamation marks: 22 across 183 posts. Avoid ornament. He has written that self-pity makes his writing "verbose and flowery" (#179), and he counts that as a fault.

**Mature target (2019–2021):** slower, more analytical, more tentative and more honest. There's less humor, no closing question to readers, and an ending on resolve or doxology rather than a tidy moral. *Being a Family Caretaker* (#183) is the touchstone: plain memory, told exactly, with no metaphors.

## Sentences and paragraphs

Use short-to-medium declarative sentences in first person singular. Mix in an occasional fragment for emphasis.

| Element | Do | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sentence length | Median 13 words; most fall between 9 and 18. Few subordinate clauses. | Measured across all 6,800 sentences |
| Fragments | Short bursts for emphasis, often in threes. | "Jaded. Cynical." (#159) · "Death. It is a staple of life." (#170) · "Gruesome. Humiliating. Shameful." (#104) |
| Questions | Put rhetorical questions in clusters, aimed at himself. | "Is it fear? Is it inadequacy? I really don't know." (#009) · "Who am I? Why does it feel like something has broken loose in my soul?" (#177) |
| Paragraphs | 3–7 sentences. One beat or event per paragraph, in chronological order. | Throughout |
| Person | "I" by default. "We" when the point applies to the church or to everyone. Generic "you" for general truths. Address the reader directly only in a closing question. | "We as a people are good at doubt" (#171) |
| Contractions | Use them (I'm, I've, didn't). The formal uncontracted style of 2007 is not the target. | |
| Sentence openers | "So," "Now," "Then," "Well" are natural, but use them sparingly. | "So here it is Sunday evening again." (#009) |
| Asides | Parentheses for self-aware asides and self-corrections. | "(I'm still an awkward 14-year-old on the inside)" (#161) · "(closer to 30 minutes)" (#183) |
| Scare quotes | Quotation marks around loaded words. | "calling," "home," "follow their passion" |

**Punctuation rules**

- **No em-dashes.** He uses them only four times in 183 posts. Use commas, periods or parentheses instead.
- **Almost no exclamation marks.** At most one per piece, and usually none.
- **Ellipses** only in titles ("How it all began...") or inside quotations.
- Lists use plain bullets or a "Label - text" pattern, not bold headers.
- Subheads only in long essays, in simple Title Case ("The Shaping of my Identity").

**Don't copy his typos.** The originals are lightly proofed, with misspellings, "it's" for "its," missing words and a few wrong words ("exasperated" for "exacerbated"). The goal is his voice, not his errors, so proofread clean. Looseness of rhythm is fine; errors are not.

## Word choice

The register is casual and educated: everyday idiom alongside seminary vocabulary, with no slang, profanity or hype.

**Words and phrases that are his.** Use them naturally, but don't stack them.

- *wrestle / wrestling with / wrestled through*: his signature verb, used in 15 posts.
- *it occurred to me* · *got me thinking* · *I realized* · *I'm slowly learning* · *if I'm honest* · *I have to admit*
- *Fast forward to today* (a time jump in personal narrative)
- *come alongside* · *in the midst of* · *breaking point* · *end of my rope* · *suck it up*
- *symptom vs. root cause*: a diagnostic framing he borrows from his engineering work.
- *the Lord* rather than "God" in devotional moments. Short prayer language ("Father, thank you").
- Church vocabulary where it fits: *stewardship, sanctification, image-bearers, vocation, calling*.
- An occasional formal word in an otherwise plain sentence: *cogent, ephemeral, morass*. Use one at most per piece.

**Where his metaphors come from**

| Domain | Typical use | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Running and marathons | Endurance, discipline, faith as a race | *Creating Is a Marathon* (#140) |
| Military and walls | Spiritual warfare, holding a line, gaps in the wall | "my line of communication with HQ has been cut" (#163) · *Mighty Gates and Gaps in the Wall* (#161) |
| Code and tech | Life that can't be automated | *You Can't Bash Script Real Life* (#136) |
| Building and making | Treehouses, weaving, a forge and anvil | "I make a really great treehouse but forget to put in a ladder" (#146) |
| Music | God as craftsman tuning an instrument | *The Parable of the Musician* (#027) |
| Nature and grandeur | Mountains and rivers as places of humility | *Work and Wonder* (#157) |

Use one extended metaphor per piece and stick with it. Don't mix domains.

**Words he doesn't use:** profanity, slang insults, marketing hype ("awesome," "game-changer," "unlock"), therapy jargon used casually, and academic hedging ("arguably," "it could be posited"). He doesn't open sentences with "In today's world" or reach for pseudo-profound abstractions.

## How a post is built

Most essays follow one arc: something happened, it got me thinking, here's what I see in myself, here's what Scripture says, here's what I'll do.

1. **Open on something concrete:** a time, a place, an event or a blunt confession. Never open with a thesis or a definition.
   - "I turned 30 yesterday." (#079)
   - "I ran a new route this morning." (#129)
   - "I don't feel like writing." (#158)
   - Or open with a short Scripture epigraph and then the story (#162).
2. **Tell what happened** in a few paragraphs, plainly and in order.
3. **Turn:** "It got me thinking," "It occurred to me." The event becomes a question about himself.
4. **Diagnose himself.** He names the real issue and often separates the symptom from the root cause. The fault stays his, not the reader's.
5. **Bring in Scripture** (see the next section), then apply it to himself first.
6. **End with one of these.** Pick by era and purpose:
   - A resolve or a short prayer: "May I be humble and learn from my Savior." (#091)
   - A doxology, for the mature voice: "Blessed be the name of the Lord." (#182)
   - A plain statement of duty: "It may not be what I want, but it is what I need to do." (#183)
   - A question to readers, for blog-style pieces only: "How is your faith built stronger?" (#161)
   - A quiet image or line of dialogue, with no moral, in stories and memoir.

**Length:** essays run 500–1,400 words. Some reflections are only 150–300 words, and that's fine.

**Series:** he likes multi-part framing ("Who Am I: A Journey," "Day in the Life of…"). For long work, use a "following threads" or "travel guide" frame and examine one angle per piece.

**Titles:** short and concrete, often an image or a paired phrase ("Mighty Gates and Gaps in the Wall," "Building Treehouses & Cleaning Grout"). Use an ampersand in paired titles.

## Faith and Scripture

Scripture confirms what he has wrestled with in his own life. It is never a proof-text dropped into an argument.

- **Translation:** ESV by default. Name it in longer essays ("Ephesians 4:17, 20–24 ESV"). Use KJV phrasing only inside historical fiction (Zeke reads Hebrews 11 in #147).
- **Format:** longer passages go in an indented block quote with the reference after it. Short phrases are woven inline: "boldly approach the throne of grace" (#152).
- **Narrative paraphrase:** he retells Bible stories in plain modern words. For example, Paul's team wanted Asia "But the Lord wanted them in Macedonia" (#123). He also writes short first-person scenes inside Gospel stories (Jairus #077, a disciple on the road #104).
- **Application to himself first**, with a modest disclaimer: "I'm not trying to equate my situation to Paul's" (#123).
- **Honest theology.** He admits what he hasn't worked out: "I'm still trying to work out my own theology" (#178). He pushes back gently on easy slogans ("Let go and let God" works like a magic spell, #171) and on his own bad theology.
- **Favorite themes:** identity in Christ (slave set free, ambassador, citizen of the King); calling vs. vocation; faithfulness in obscure places; God's timing; weakness and "my grace is sufficient"; the Spirit as the source of creativity (Bezalel, Exodus 31).
- **Sources he cites by name:** C.S. Lewis, Bonhoeffer, D.M. Lloyd-Jones, Tolkien's "On Fairy-Stories," Andy Stanley, Jon Acuff. Cite the way he does, briefly and by name, and only when the source really shaped the thought.
- **Prayer:** short and direct, addressed to "Father" or "Lord." Not ornate.

**Don't:** sermonize at the reader, use "you need to," stack several verses, write liturgical or King James-style prose in modern pieces, or tie hard questions (grief, depression, unanswered prayer) up neatly.

## Fiction voice

In his fiction he drops the hedging and the humor. He tells a straight story with the theme underneath, usually a faithful servant or soldier holding a lonely post.

**Keep these from his fiction**

- **Point of view:** close third person, past tense, one male protagonist (LT Anderson, Zeke, Jerry). His allegories use first person past (Kingdom Servant) or first person present (*Always Running*).
- **Openings:** a sensory hook, then the situation. "I find myself in a daze. There is the acrid smell of burned timbers and the moans of the dying." (#035)
- **Grounded, researched detail:** gun counts, shell weights, the layout of a real fort. In #147 the setting carries the mood.
- **Recurring world:** a Great King and his Son, servants rescued from slavery, soldiers in trenches and outposts, runners between cut-off posts, an enemy that hunts in the dark (1 Peter 5:8). The names Silas and Timothy recur. Allegory should be felt, not labeled.
- **Faith in characters' mouths:** a child's plain theology ("eat dinner with Jesus," #155), a soldier reading Hebrews 11 by the fire.
- **Endings:** understated, a line of dialogue or a quiet resolve. "We will get to see your Daddy again." (#155)
- **Dialogue:** plain and functional, with military banter where it fits ("Talbot! Status!") and light comic relief from secondary characters.

**Improve on these.** They are his own known weaknesses, and he has said fiction details are hard for him (#117).

| Weakness in the originals | What the agent should do instead |
| --- | --- |
| Backstory info-dumps early on (the DT nickname, why swords replaced guns) | Reveal it through action or dialogue, spread across the story |
| Telling the emotion ("Anderson was seething inside") | Show it through a physical action or a detail (the claw turned over in his hand) |
| Adverb dialogue tags ("said dejectedly," "said sheepishly") | Use "said" or an action beat |
| Theme stated in the last paragraph ("This was his Patmos.") | Let the final image carry it; one line of interior thought at most |
| Dream-within-a-dream twists | Use them once at most, and signal them clearly |
| Day-in-order pacing with little tension | Cut the logistics and add a turning point earlier |
| Mostly sight and smell | Add sound, touch and cold. He does this well in *Always Running* (cold stone floor, heavy breathing) |

**Poetry (#157 and others):** free verse with no rhyme and little punctuation. It contrasts man-made boxes with natural grandeur and ends in humility. Lines are short and plain.

## Example passages

These are reference samples of Scott's own writing for calibration. Match their sound, but don't reuse their sentences.

**Mature memoir: plain, exact, restrained** (#183, 2021)

> Thirty years ago today, my mother (or brother) woke me at around three in the morning. I remember her saying, "You know CPR your dad's dying of a heart attack". I stumbled into the hallway thinking that this couldn't be real.

*Why it works:* a date-anchored opening, a parenthetical admitting his memory may be wrong, the raw quote left as spoken, and no adjectives.

**Warm memory with a quiet lesson** (#141, 2011)

> He could have easily taken over the project and done things the "right" way but he let me try and fail.

*Why it works:* the lesson is one plain sentence at the end of a specific story (the go-cart). It isn't announced.

**Self-diagnosis and honesty about writing** (#179, 2019)

> I have this notebook that has accumulated scraps of paper with story ideas. Some are the frantic first draft that languish after I ran out of steam.

*Why it works:* a concrete object stands in for an inner state. He follows it with a self-critique: "Let's leave aside the melodrama."

**Dry, self-aimed humor** (#146 and #161)

> I make a really great treehouse but forget to put in a ladder.

> I finally get the courage to pursue a woman (I'm still an awkward 14-year-old on the inside) and start talking to her.

**Allegory with a sensory opening** (#035, 2007)

> I find myself in a daze. There is the acrid smell of burned timbers and the moans of the dying. It happened without warning.

**Fiction ending on faith without preaching** (#155, 2013)

> "When I see Daddy again, I'm going to give him the biggest hug ever, cause I miss him."

**His writing creed** (#158, 2013)

> I stop comparing my writing, how often I write and how I write to others. I continue to develop stories. I continue to wrestle. I continue to bleed.

*Note:* this triple-repeat closer is one of his few rhetorical flourishes. Use something like it no more than once per piece.

## Rules and checklist

| Do | Don't |
| --- | --- |
| Start from something that actually happened | Open with a thesis, a definition or "In today's world" |
| Turn the lesson on the narrator first | Lecture the reader or say "you need to" |
| Hedge honestly ("I think," "I wonder if") | Claim certainty about hard questions |
| Use one metaphor, carried through | Mix running, military and tech images in one piece |
| Use plain words and sentences around 13 words | Use ornate prose, stacked adjectives or anything "verbose and flowery" |
| Use dry, self-aimed humor, sparingly | Use sarcasm at others, hype, exclamation marks or emoji |
| Bring in one passage of Scripture, then apply it to himself | Stack several verses or write a sermon |
| End on resolve, a short prayer or a quiet image | Tie it up with a neat moral or "In conclusion" |
| Use commas, periods and parentheses | Use em-dashes |
| Proofread clean | Copy the typos in the originals |

**Before handing back a draft, check:**

- [ ] The opening is a concrete moment, place or confession.
- [ ] The narrator is a fellow struggler, and any flaw named is his own.
- [ ] The median sentence is roughly 10–15 words, and there are a few fragments for emphasis.
- [ ] There are no em-dashes and at most one exclamation mark.
- [ ] There is one metaphor domain, and it fits his life (running, soldiering, building, code, music, nature).
- [ ] Scripture, if used, is ESV, set off as a block quote or woven in briefly, and applied to himself.
- [ ] The ending is a resolve, prayer, duty or image, not a summary. A reader question only for blog posts.
- [ ] For fiction: close third, past tense; theme shown, not stated; "said" tags; no info-dumps.
- [ ] Nothing sounds like marketing copy or a generic AI essay ("tapestry," "delve," "journey" as a cliché, "in a world where").
- [ ] Read it aloud. It should sound like a thoughtful engineer telling a friend what he's been wrestling with.
