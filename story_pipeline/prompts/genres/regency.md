# Regency voice

## The four things that make it sound right

**1. Free indirect discourse.** The narrator slips into a character's thoughts
without announcing it, so the reader cannot always tell whether a judgement
belongs to the character or the book. This is the most identifiable feature of
the style and the one most often missing from imitations.

> She had been perfectly certain of his indifference. It was not to be borne
> that she should have been wrong about a thing so plain.

Not: *She thought to herself that she had been certain of his indifference.*

**2. Irony carried by the gap between what is said and what is true.** The
narrator does not editorialize. She reports a character's self-assessment in
that character's own inflated terms and lets the reader do the arithmetic. If
you write a sentence telling the reader someone is ridiculous, delete it and let
them speak instead.

**3. Dialogue does the work.** Characters reveal themselves by the length of
their sentences, what they decline to say, and whether they answer the question
asked. A foolish character talks too long. A guarded one answers a question with
a question. A well-bred one insults by agreeing.

**4. Money and rank are load-bearing.** Every relationship runs through income,
entail, connection, and the precise distance between two families. A proposal is
a financial event. Name the numbers — a living worth two hundred a year, a
fortune of ten thousand pounds — because period readers priced everything.

## Diction

Period-plausible, not archaic. This prose is closer to modern English than people
expect. Nothing should jar; not every word must be old.

- **No anachronism after roughly 1820:** railways, photographs, gas lighting in a
  country house, tinned food, "okay", "teenager", "job", "boyfriend", "date" as a
  social engagement.
- **No Elizabethan cosplay.** "Forsooth", "prithee", "milady", "methinks" are two
  centuries early and mark the writing as costume immediately.
- **Titles used correctly.** Miss Bennet is the eldest unmarried daughter; her
  younger sisters are Miss Elizabeth Bennet. A knight is Sir John, never Sir
  Thompson. The informed part of the audience leaves over this.
- **Long balanced sentences punctuated by a short flat one** that lands the joke.

## Not pastiche

Original fiction in this manner. No character, place, or sentence from an actual
Austen novel appears. Her text is public domain; lifting it still produces
something a reader recognizes and stops reading.
