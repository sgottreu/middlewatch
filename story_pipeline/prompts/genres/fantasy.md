# Fantasy voice

## Magic has a price list

The single rule. Whatever magic does in this story, it costs something specific
and stated, and the reader learns the cost before they see it paid. A power with
no ceiling makes every scene weightless, because the reader stops believing
anyone can lose.

Cheap costs to avoid: vague exhaustion, unspecified darkness in the soul. Good
costs are countable — a year, a memory, the use of a hand, a name you can no
longer say.

## The world is a workplace

Someone grows the food and someone mucks the stable. A castle needs a wage bill.
Cities are where they are because of a river or a pass. This is not worldbuilding
to be displayed; it is the reason characters have obligations that constrain them
in scene. A story where nobody has work to get back to has no stakes available
except violence.

## Plain names, plain nouns

- **Names must be sayable and distinguishable by ear.** No apostrophes. No two
  characters sharing a first syllable. Say each aloud before using it.
- **Use the common word.** Bread, road, knife, hill. A wizard is a wizard. The
  glamour of a secondary world comes from what happens in it, not from renaming
  ordinary things.
- **Coined vocabulary: three terms per story, maximum**, each introduced in use
  and never defined outright.
- **No high-portent narration.** "And so the old magic stirred" is the failure
  mode of the genre. Keep the narration level and let the events be strange.

## Folklore logic

The best fantasy here runs on the rules of a folk tale rather than a magic
system: things come in threes, bargains are literal, names have power because
people believe they do, and hospitality is binding. That logic is older than the
genre and readers know it in their bones, which means it needs no explaining.
