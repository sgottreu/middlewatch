# House rules

Universal across every genre. The genre file supplies the voice; this file
supplies the shape.

## Written to be heard

Every word is spoken aloud by a synthetic voice over a still image. That changes
what works.

- A listener cannot re-read. One idea per sentence in the complicated passages.
- **A chapter's first sentence has to stand on its own.** It is where a listener
  pauses, where an ad break lands, and where someone returns after a day away.
  *It should be understood that Mr Rowe had a reason* asks them to remember
  which reason, from two beats back in the previous chapter, past an intervening
  scene about something else. Name the thing: *had not forgiven Miss Vane for the
  business of the verse* costs six words and works cold.
- **Do not use the story's own objects as metaphors.** Whatever the plot turns
  on — a cracked beam, a disputed will, a dispensing book — owns its words
  literally, and every figurative use of them sends the listener to the wrong
  place. *He had kept it in good repair since November* is a fine sentence about
  a grievance in any other story; in this one the schoolroom roof needs fifteen
  pounds of repair, and *repair* is spoken for. Pick a different image.
- **Coordinate at most two clauses in a sentence.** *He said the damp had got in,
  and had been getting in for years, and that he had mentioned it once* is three,
  and by the third the listener has lost the subject of the first. Split it, or
  hold every member to the same grammatical shape so the ear can follow the
  pattern. A long sentence is not the fault; a long sentence that changes shape
  halfway is.
- **Names must be distinguishable by ear.** No two characters beginning with the
  same syllable, no rhymes, no two with the same length and stress pattern. Say
  every name aloud before committing to it.
- Dialogue attribution goes at the start or in a natural pause, not trailing
  three clauses later. The listener needs to know who is speaking before they
  have heard the line.
- No nested parenthetical clauses. They read beautifully and listen terribly.
- Numbers get rounded. Long digit strings are unhearable.

**A jump inside a chapter is marked, not narrated.** A break segment prints
`* * *` on the page and becomes a long silence in the narration. The mark is
not the cue — silence alone tells a listener nothing — so the sentence that
follows one names the new time or the new place. Anything longer than a night
or wider than the parish is usually a chapter, not a break.

**One voice reads everything.** The narration is a single reader performing the
whole story, as an audiobook is, not a cast with a microphone each. So the prose
carries who is speaking — an unattributed line in a scene of three is lost the
moment it is heard rather than read — and nothing in the writing should depend on
two characters sounding different.

## Tropes

Every story declares two or three tropes from its genre's list, and the outline
records them. They are a promise to the audience: someone who clicked a
marriage-of-convenience story gets one, delivered, not subverted into something
else.

- **Two or three. Never more.** Four or more stacked is where a story stops being
  a story and becomes a checklist, and it is the most common failure here.
- **At least one is delivered straight.** The reader came for it. Give it to
  them.
- **A second may be inverted**, but only if the inversion is itself satisfying
  rather than merely withheld. Setting up a trope and refusing to pay it off is
  not cleverness.
- **The trope is the frame, not the plot.** "Marriage of convenience" tells you
  the situation. It does not tell you who these two people are, what either of
  them wants that the other cannot give, or what the specific thing is that
  breaks in chapter four. That is still the ideator's job.
- Tropes are never named in the prose. No character thinks about being in a
  marriage of convenience.
